To waver in opinion or judgment; to be 
in uncertainty as to belief respecting anything; to hesitate in belief; to be 
undecided as to the truth of the negative or the affirmative proposition; to be undetermined. %%retain formatting of dangling 'to be', which is referenced below%%

What is doubt? Excellent question! First, doubt is one of [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances|the Five Hindrances]] to #concentration in the practice of Buddhist #meditation. But before we get to the first thing as it relates to applied Buddhism, a detour into [[epistemology]], my favourite! 

My initial formatting in the above definition inadvertently revealed something significant ~ *to be, is to doubt*. In epistemology, (I would feel confident in saying) doubt is a prerequisite for knowledge ~ or, perhaps, for *true* knowledge. One might believe they know something, and have no doubt ~ this would be a nightmare, entrenched certainty that cannot be modified because there is no room for new evidence to be considered. Without doubt, no one would ever question what they *think* is true, but perhaps or probably isn't. So we can believe something to be true, without a doubt, and be completely wrong because doubt is not there as a check-and-balance. When doubt is present we can feel more confident that our knowledge is at least moving toward [[justified true belief]], even if we aren't certain about something we believe to be true.

So, doubt is a good thing. Don't be worried if you are in doubt ~ it just means that you are open to being wrong, to encountering new evidence that might displace your current belief.

Descartes famously declared, "I think, therefore I am." According to my current understanding, he said this because the only thing he could certain of is that he was thinking ~ therefore, his beingness was embedded in thinking, could not be separated from thinking. Buddhism tells a different story, thankfully: if I identified with every fleeting and inane thought that came to mind, I would be a blubbering mess; indeed, I *was* a blubbering mess for quite a long time as an adolescent and young adult, precisely because I hadn't yet learnt that great maxim of self-help and truth-seekers the world over: *You are not your thoughts.* So there, #Descartes was wrong. Phew! 

So far, in answer to our question, 'What is (the nature of) doubt?', we have at least some understanding: doubt is good, not necessarily a problem; the presence of doubt helps us to move closer to truth. Considering that, let's pivot back to [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances | doubt in the Five Hindrances]].   
# Doubt in the Five Hindrances
In the #practice of meditation, the Buddha identified five common hindrances to concentration: sensual desire, ill will, restlessness & worry, sloth & torpor, and doubt. Considering doubt is one of the hindrances to concentration, and concentration is a prerequisite for #insight, it is important that we get a handle on doubt. In his book, TITLE, Rahula (3) makes it clear that (spiritual) progress depends on eliminating doubt, and as we have seen elsewhere, faith as the opposite of doubt is fundamental for that same progress. I am stating the obvious, but with the intention of highlighting something like a false dichotomy: as argued above, doubt is *good*, but *faith is also good*. Is this another paradox? 

Another paradox to consider is that freedom from doubt depends on one seeing clearly in the first place, which would typically be understand as the source of faith: indeed, the sotapanna has eliminated doubt and arrived at 'unshakeable' faith because they have seen the Four Noble Truths directly for themselves.

So where do we start here? Is this a chicken, an egg, or an ouroboros? Does doubt move us toward truth, or is faith the prime mover here? If the process of truth-seeking is more like an ouroboros, then all I need to do is put my head up my arse. Instead, I guess we need to unpack the types of doubt we are talking about, because distinctions like that could make all the difference.

One kind of doubt is doubt in the Triple Gem. Another is doubt in one's own capacity to follow the Buddha's instructions, understand his teachings, and apply oneself to the practice of the noble ones.
