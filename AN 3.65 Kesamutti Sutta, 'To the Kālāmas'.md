In [AN 3.65](https://study.jhana.info/suttas/an3.65), the Buddha advises a confused group of practitioners who are often subjected to teachers of various competing doctrines. He says there are ten ways that are not sufficient for discerning whether a teaching is dhamma, or not: 

- by oral tradition
- by lineage
- by hearsay
- by reliance on a collection of scriptures
- by logical reasoning
- by inferential reasoning
- by reasoned cogitation
- by acceptance after pondering
- by the apparent competence (or charisma) of the teacher
- by the teacher's position as your guru
### Oral Tradition
This is straightforward enough - don't accept a teaching simply on the grounds it was received through tradition. 
### Lineage
It is interesting for me to see here again that lineage is neither sufficient, nor required for accepting a teaching as dhamma - like every other method here, it is secondary to experience. 

I was hung up on lineage for a good while, but the Kalama Sutta helps me understand I am better of appraising a teacher and their teaching by their conduct, because that is the only measure for a teaching in this sutta anyway - does it reduce greed, hatred, and delusion? Then it is dhamma. Sounds reasonable to me ✅

I took lineage to be of central importance alongside the importance of one's teacher having a teacher, but these are subjective compared to the direct experience of the wholesome, etc. and reduced suffering. Someone like %%A. Ariya%% might have trained a lot under various teachers and be independent now, but clearly further down the path so he may not need a lot of guidance. A practitioner after a certain stage[^1] of progress is trusted by the Buddha as no longer needing supervision.
### Hearsay
Taking anything to be true on the grounds of hearsay is clearly unreliable, especially when the proponents are not free of the kilesas themselves - the blind men trying to discern the elephant in the room. %%I am not sure if this is in this sutta, or Canki, but they are related enough anyway%%
### Collections
I suppose 'collections' means a *selection* of the suttas deemed essential or all that is required - or this refers to groups/practitioners who had access to only limited suttas, as was the case for most of history. So we are in a unique position to not be taken in by teachings based on certain sets of scriptures - or, worse, by teachings that discourage reading the suttas for oneself. 

With websites like [Sutta Central](https://suttacentral.now), [Pāli Canon Study Tool](https://study.jhana.info), and [The Buddha's Words](https://www.thebuddhaswords.net/home/index.html), there is no reason we should get caught by this. 
### Logical Reasoning
The next four all looked the same to me, and I understand as much as conceptual reasoning alone is not sufficient for discerning whether a teaching dhamma. 

In the context of *saddhā* it seems almost conflicting that the Kalama Sutta seems to denounce the sort of reasoning I thought would support 'conviction' or 'confidence', these ideals of 'reasoned faith' or 'faith with wisdom', but the Buddha is just saying *these are not sufficient reason* to accept a teaching: the only sufficient reason is evidence from the sense realm that suffering is reduced. 

He does not discourage reasoning but gives it very specific direction - that is, considering whether conduct suggested by that teaching is 

> wholesome
> blameworthy
> censured by the wise, and
> conducive to harm and suffering

So, is the teaching skilful? The answer is found in our conduct. The Buddha encourages us to use reasoning for the examination of experience rather than just treating intellectual persuasiveness as a sufficient reason for accepting a teaching. After all, reasoning can fail, as recognised in the [[MN 95 Caṅkīsutta, 'With Caṅkī'|Canki Sutta]].
