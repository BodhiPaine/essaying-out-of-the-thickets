**doubt** (daʊt)[^1] *intransitive verb*, rhymes with “out”; silent ‘b’
To waver in opinion or judgment; to be 
in uncertainty as to belief respecting anything; to hesitate in belief; to be 
undecided as to the truth of the negative or the affirmative proposition; to be undetermined. %%retain formatting of dangling 'to be', which is referenced below%%

What is doubt? Excellent question! First, doubt is one of [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances|the Five Hindrances]] to #concentration in the practice of Buddhist #meditation. But before we get to the first thing as it relates to applied Buddhism, a detour into [[epistemology]], my favourite! 

My initial formatting in the above definition inadvertently revealed something significant ~ *to be, is to doubt*. In #epistemology  (I would feel confident in saying) doubt is a prerequisite for knowledge ~ or, perhaps, for *true* knowledge. One might believe they know something, and have no doubt ~ this would be a nightmare, entrenched certainty that cannot be modified because there is no room for new evidence to be considered. Without doubt, no one would ever question what they *think* is true, but perhaps or probably isn't. So we can believe something to be true, without a doubt, and be completely wrong because doubt is not there as a check-and-balance. When doubt is present we can feel more confident that our knowledge is at least moving toward [[justified true belief]], even if we aren't certain about something we believe to be true.

So, doubt is a good thing. Don't be worried if you are in doubt ~ it just means that you are open to being wrong, to encountering new evidence that might displace your current belief. You're not alone in this, and this aspect of the #human-condition even has a name, 'epistemic self-doubt'[^3]. In fact if you are one of the few who are aware of their own epistemic self-doubt, you are in good company. Roush[^2] explains on *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* how Socrates perfected this. He was famously confident that he didn't know, and would 'go around town asking others for their answers and making awkward observations about their replies'. When he was infamously prosecuted and murdered for such brazen acknowledgement of the [[ignorance]] we all share, he told his prosecuters that his self-proclaimed ignorance 'had the salutary effect of making it possible for him to find out' because 'if he were sure that he already knew, then he would not have motivation to look for the answer'. 

So I didn't just make up all that stuff above, and if you are reading this then we are both part of an long and esteemed tradition of not-knowing. I feel grateful that somehow I have always been intuitively aware of this, and have found myself saying on occasion that 'ignorance is the gateway to knowledge'. I wasn't always grateful for this, because it made me awkward company pretty much anywhere I went in the suburbs of Adelaide at the time.

Descartes, on the other hand, famously declared, "I think, therefore I am." Two truth claims there: it is 'I' who thinks; and 'I' am. Neither of these claims hold water under the rubric of [[epistemology#Buddhist Epistemology|Buddhist epistemology]] as I understand it. According to my current understanding of [[epistemology#Cartesian epistemology|Cartesian epistemology]], Descarte said *cogito ergo sum* because the only thing he could be certain of is that he was thinking ~ therefore, his beingness was embedded in thinking, could not be separated from thinking. Buddhism tells a different story, thankfully: if I identified with every fleeting and inane thought that came to mind, I would be a blubbering mess; indeed, I *was* a blubbering mess for quite a long time as an adolescent and young adult, precisely because I hadn't yet learnt that great maxim of self-help and truth-seekers the world over: *You are not your thoughts.* So there, #Descartes was wrong. Phew! 

So far, in answer to our question, 'What is (the nature of) doubt?', we have at least some understanding: doubt is good, not necessarily a problem; the presence of doubt helps us to move closer to truth. Considering that, let's pivot back to [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances | doubt in the Five Hindrances]]. 
# Doubt in the Five Hindrances
In the #practice of meditation, the Buddha identified five common hindrances to concentration: sensual desire, ill will, restlessness & worry, sloth & torpor, and doubt. Considering doubt is one of the hindrances to concentration, and concentration is a prerequisite for #insight, it is important that we get a handle on doubt. In his book, *[[What the Buddha Taught]]*, Rahula (3) makes it clear that (spiritual) progress depends on eliminating doubt, and as we have seen elsewhere, faith as the opposite of doubt is fundamental for that same progress. I am stating the obvious, but with the intention of highlighting something like a false dichotomy: as argued above, doubt is *good*, but *faith is also good*. Is this another paradox? 

Another paradox to consider is that freedom from doubt depends on one seeing clearly in the first place, which would typically be understand as the source of faith: indeed, the sotapanna has eliminated doubt and arrived at 'unshakeable' faith because they have seen the Four Noble Truths directly for themselves.

So where do we start here? Is this a chicken, an egg, or an ouroboros? Does doubt move us toward truth, or is faith the prime mover here? If the process of truth-seeking is more like an ouroboros, then all I need to do is put my head up my arse. Instead, I guess we need to unpack the types of doubt we are talking about, because distinctions like that could make all the difference.

One kind of doubt is doubt in the Triple Gem. Another is doubt in one's own capacity to follow the Buddha's instructions, understand his teachings, and apply oneself to the practice of the noble ones. Another kind of doubt, mentioned above, is epistemic self-doubt, meaning doubt about 'our ability to achieve an epistemically favorable state, for example, to achieve true beliefs' (Roush). But the subject here is [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances]] ~ stay with it, Bodhi.

If one has doubt in the sense of the Hindrances, it means, quite specifically, doubt about the Triple Gem: did the Buddha really become enlightened and then go on as a peripatetic pedagogue 'asking others for their answers and making awkward observations about their replies' the way Socrates did, which is not to say Socrates was enlightened, but 🤷 who knows; do the teachings of the Buddha really contain instructions we can follow to experience enlightenment for ourselves; and is the community of Buddha's disciples still alive and applying themselves with #sincerity to the application of those teachings? 

It does sound a bit like a stretch, when I type it all out. And for a long time, I wasn't sure. Caveat: I do happen to feel sure about this now; my faith in the Triple Gem is pretty solid. The main kind of doubt I experience is that second one: I sometimes feel unsure about whether I've got what it takes to understand and apply the teachings all the way to the final goal. I also have all sorts of epistemic self-doubt, but Buddha's teaching about the Unprofitable Questions helps with that.

So that's where we're at with that, for now. %%give it a brush up, then push it imperfect%%

## One-Method Approaches
There is this idea in some circles that only one method is required ~ a practitioner achieves some degree of success with a method, then becomes a teacher of that method: many folk don’t want to make the effort to understand the suttas themselves, and I understand the temptation to just trust someone we hope is the expert they claim to be. It’s a whole thing.

Doubt can arise if one has encountered something like Goenka and the they are not progressing with the sole meditation technique. If you only have a hammer, everything begins to look like a nail.

This one is an easy doubt to resolve with recourse to the [[simile of the cook]], to the famous [[MN 20]], and to [[AN 6.58 Āsavasutta, 'Defilements'|AN 6.58]] *Āsavasutta*, ’Defilements’, where the Buddha explains that ‘diverse methods should be used for overcoming diverse kinds of problems’ and that ’one who is skilled in this is “worthy of offerings”’. #sanghānussati

## Fear of Credulity
Soma makes the consoling observation, citing D I 34, that ‘criticism in the sense of careful judgment belongs to the fundamentals of the doctrine and is necessary for reaching right understanding (*sammādiṭṭhi*)’, such that 'a teaching should not be accepted by a reasonable person without investigation, particularly if he wants to live in conformity with it’. ’Study of a teaching through inquiring into it is unfavourable to #credulity; but such study may produce #faith in the teaching if it is true and intelligible.’

Remaining unquestioningly attached to the worldview I inherited is the sort of credulity I should fear, rather than fearing I will be perceived as credulous for my willingness to believe in a Buddhist worldview by so-called rational materialists or other folk who hold themselves to be sophisticated because they have heard about modern science.

How many modern-day science zealots have you met whose inquiry into their own beliefs have been unfavourable to credulity? Very few, I would hazard to guess ~ because unquestioned attachment to the worldview we inherited is a much more comfortable epistemological terrain to inhabit than the space of uncertainty one finds themselves hurtling through after they have let go of one worldview and have yet to find another, %%which requires developing the trapeze analogy%%

Meanwhile, it still bugs me sometimes that I’m not sure how I feel about such claims as being able to swim in the Earth %%MN 11?%%, perhaps because I have read this depicted in an actual sci-fi novel ~ brilliant book though, and part of a trilogy by %%what’s here name?%% Is it possible that a man from ancient India could use his refined mind to defy laws of physics we take as gospel today? Do worldviews influence the way laws of physics operate across different cultures in time? If the ancient Indian worldview included/permitted human abilities a modern Western worldview would chalk up as paranormal ~ improbable at best, and flagrantly impossible at worst ~ then being able to swim in the Earth would not be in defiance of natural law, but in compliance with a perception we have lost, marginalised into the 'unstudyable' [[the Hard Problem of Consciousness|too-hard basket]]. 

Does it have to be as dichotomous as all this, as Patrick Harpur suggests with his imploration that we re-cultivate a sense of 'double-vision', the capacity to see mythology as existing somewhere in the blurry cross-eyed epistemological state of being simultaneously metaphorical, and literal[^5]. Can these claims be dismissed as embellishments venerating an already mythologised (literally lionised) man?, while retaining the pragmatic[^4] instructions and putting the mythological, metaphysical, and cosmology details aside, chalking them up as ... what, superstition? If these are *just cultural embellishments*, then his ability to abandon suffering may be also, which would tank/scuttle the whole thing. That's the sort of reasoning that my mind gets into around this. 

I consider reading the likes of [Dan Ingram](https://www.integrateddaniel.info/book/), and other folk who claim to have proved that the dhamma works, by becoming arahants. I also find it helpful to read contemporary dhamma-related authors who speak my language, and researching where modern science is beginning to converge with the insights of the Buddha, as well researching philosophy of science to understand that, you know, that inherited worldview: much of it is founded upon assumptions that themselves are taken on faith. ### The Bodhisattva Ideal
I am listing this under Incompatible Worldview because the Mahayana ideal of the bodhisattva is an interpretation of Buddhist cosmology that is not found in Early Buddhism - it was *added* to the cosmology later, and holding it or dropping it is a choice. If holding belief in the bodhisattva ideal [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#What is Dhamma, and aDhamma?|contradicts the dhamma]] then it needs to be abandoned, and the stakes are high here - if one chooses to renounce nirvana on behalf of all sentient beings, they may be subjecting themselves to endless rounds of rebirth until the impossible or at least unimaginable goal of all beings escaping the cycle of rebirth ... what happens when there are no more beings in the cycle of rebirth? 👀 🤯 This question honestly troubles me. 
## Internal Consistency of the Dhamma
The question of internal consistency may need to be considered as well: 

a. if one thing doesn't make sense or seems inconsistent with another, should that necessarily compromise my faith in the rest of the teachings? 

b. does a requirement/expectation for such consistency come from the cultural conditioning of someone steeped in linear logic and can this be let go of/replaced by another kind of logic, or the cultivation of #double-vision?
