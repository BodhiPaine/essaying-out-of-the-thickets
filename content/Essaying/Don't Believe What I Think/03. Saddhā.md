Let's begin this chapter by pointing out the elephant in the room: ‘faith’ is almost a dirty word in the minds of many secular folk, especially modern Westerners like myself, and especially because it is tinged with religiosity. No rational person wants their credibility to be tainted by being perceived as faithful, where 'faith' is taken to mean 'belief without evidence'. In dhamma practice, faith has a different function (more like 'conviction' or 'confidence'), as denoted by the Pali, *saddhā*. [[What the Buddha Taught by Walpola Rahula|Rahula]] (8) has a nice way of putting it: 

> Almost all religions are built on faith—rather 'blind' faith it would seem. But in Buddhism emphasis is laid on 'seeing', knowing, understanding, and not on faith, or belief. In Buddhist texts there is a word *saddhā* (Skt. *sraddha*) which is usually translated as 'faith' or 'belief'. But saddha is not 'faith' as such, but rather 'confidence' born out of conviction.

Rahula says it is "'confidence' born out of conviction", and I know this is better than blind faith because it requires considering the evidence or claims or arguments to discern whether they are reasonable, but conviction is still a kind of faith. It is certainly a step short of knowledge or direct experience, but such faith is more like a reasoned decision to trust or believe the Buddha’s teachings, with awareness that I need to check the claims for myself, which of course the Buddha encouraged. 

Importantly, he didn’t encourage us to just appraise the claims conceptually, but said *ehi-passika*, 'come and see', not come and believe. Rather than demanding blind belief, the Path is more like starting with lightly held belief, and seeing where it takes you. #faith 

The Buddha never asked us to commit the sort of 'blind belief' that makes our local half-(un)conscious metaphysical materialist relatively okay with bleeding from the anus due to unknown causes, but never from the eyes. Forget about statues bleeding from the eyes! Just a hallucination. What the Buddha did ask of us ~ nay, demanded, sometimes with a stern admonishment ~ is that we hear the dhamma, reflect upon the dhamma, apply the dhamma, and see for oneself, *==ehipassika==*,%%what are the sutta references  for this?%% whether dhamma is true, or not.

'Seeing is believing,' we say, because we haven't been with Buddhism for long enough as a culture to understand that the mind itself is also a [[sense faculty]], and one that can 'see' things the eyes cannot. Considering the mind as a sense faculty in its own right though, requires a pivot: a sharp turn to avoid any quippyness about Bruce Willis telling bedtime stories and \[==his charge==] whispering, "I see dead people." 

Because the point here is just that faith doesn't have to be scary, anymore than ignorance has to be shameful: we've all got one (a mind), and I hope in this essay to deepen my own conviction, at least, that learning how to direct our attention with what I am now deciding to call 'skilful faith' might leaven our attachment to being afraid of feeling stupid for choosing to believe in something that might at least be liberative ~ considering we already do faith all, the, time, on the basis of many things we might never be able to know directly for ourselves. # Faith in Buddhism
Faith in Buddhism is different from what we have come to understand about faith in the context of religion - looking at the word *saddhā* helps to clear this up, and and to see how confidence and conviction have a central place in Buddhist practice from [[the Five Hindrances|the five hindrances]] through the [[04. The Five Faculties|five faculties]] and all the way to [[Dependent Liberation|dependent liberation]]. 

Without faith we wouldn't have the motivation to strive - we would have no reason to apply effort or persist[^9] in the practice.
