The Buddha taught dhamma, which is a four-word clause that unpacks into a vast and sprawling doctrinal scripture and framework for directly experiencing those aspects of reality that eliminate suffering in the [[human conditon]]. Which seems like a lot, but the further one gets into the fray, the clearer things become - patterns emerge, and understanding begins to converge on those parts of the sprawl that are relevant in the application of the dhamma, which is different from the dhamma itself - the dhamma doesn't do the work for us, anymore than I can have [[your conversation at the park with my friend]]. 

A turning point for me in cultivating *saddhā* was when I started discovering that the Buddha variously taught about how to appraise both his teachings and the teachings of others - famously, in the [[Kalama Sutta]]. So we have *dhamma* and *adhamma*, that which is *not the dhamma*. An important distinction to make, obviously, but how? How do we get to the heart of what the Buddha taught, and how can we discern whether our contemporary understanding of the mind and reality are #dhamma-aligned, or not.

# What the Buddha Taught
%%find a place in the outline for this%%
An important aspect of cultivating *saddhā*, hopefully obviously, is understanding what the Buddha taught, and didn't teach. We need to understand who the Buddha was, what he taught, and how to practice in pursuit of the spiritual development he described, from full-blown ignorant worldling through the four stages of awakening to fully awakened arahant. 

We can start with a simple but extraordinarily helpful teaching found in the somewhat famous [[MN 20]], about how to deal with distracting thoughts. Can you imagine the thrill of #relief I felt upon learning there was a whole sutta dedicated to this?! And then! I later learnt there is something called [[#The Thought Trilogy]], with MN 18 and MN 19 both dealing with this subject. Thought Trilogy, I love it. 

But also, to get our priorities straight we can start with Buddha's exhortation that we pursue [[#Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering|full awakening]] in this lifetime. 
## Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering 🎯
The Buddha's teachings do have an ultimate direction, a single aim, with a [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#*Ekāyano Maggo*, The 'Direct Path'|path leading only in that direction]], and that is to *liberate oneself from suffering*. That is the #ultimate-destination, and to get there we're going to need [[#The Four Noble Truths|a map]], the Four Noble Truths. 

 The objective of Buddhism is to eliminate the suffering caused by the Three Poisons, greed, aversion, and delusion, by training our mind to see reality clearly. We do this be strengthening our concentration and using that clarity to investigate reality so that we cease conflating the impermanent with the permanent and suffering with happiness, by becoming awakened at the level of an arahant, who no longer filters reality through [[#The Defilements]]. 
 
 Awakening in Buddhism is freedom from greed, aversion, and ignorance. It can’t be described beyond that, as far as I know - the best we can do is treat it like a reverse-negative image: we describe it by describing what it isn’t - that is, awakening is the absence of greed, aversion, and delusion. It is the absence of suffering, that is the objective of Buddhism.  

Buddha described a series of meaningful benchmarks along the Path from full-blown ignorant worldling through the four stages of awakening to fully awakened arahant, and learning to home in on what is required for the early stages has helped me to access a few small fruits from applying the method the Buddha described, which begets confidence.

Once a practitioner has faith arising from direct experience of the fruits of practice, they are empowered to embark on the Path with confidence, begetting energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom - the five faculties. Without knowing where we're going, it can be hard to remain confident we can get there, so I find it helpful to check-in sometimes on the teaching that awakening is a real possibility, a genuine aspect of our human potential that is accessible to anyone who wants to make the effort. 

Once a practitioner cracks into the four stages of awakening, as a sotapanna it is said their faith becomes 'unshakeable' and they are destined ultimate awakening within the next seven years.

Reading from the Buddha such quantified statements around the subject and experience of awakening makes me a bit wary because elsewhere he talks about the [[the Ten Unprofitable Questions|maddening futility]] of trying to understand the internal mechanisms of kamma. But I remain inspired by the well-described and -trodden paths out of ignorance, especially after confirming recently I had progressed to higher stages of meditation without knowing the canonical term for the experience. 

These preliminary benchmarks on the progress bar of meditation practice are the early indicators one's practice is developing in the right way, and something worth contemplating if one is experiencing #doubt - reflecting on previous meditation progress, and on the teachings about [[the potential for awakening]]. %%find the kind of suttas that talk about having the same senses as the Buddha, every human has the potential for awakening%% 

In this section, I would like to elaborate on the Path and stages as I understand them, from access concentration through sotapanna to the arahant, piece by piece in the fashion of a [[About#About Digital Gardening|digital garden]].

### MN 20
In this sutta, Buddha teaches five methods for dealing with distracting thoughts: 

1. replace the unwholesome with the wholesome; if that doesn't work,
2. examine the danger of unwholesome thoughts; if that doesn't work, 
3. try to forget or ignore the thoughts ~ don't give them #attention; if that doesn't work, 
4. still the thought formations; if that doesn't work, 
5. crush mind with mind

Sujato points out that this single sutta alone dispels the myth that there is a single meditation method like a once-size-fits-all hard hat on the building site of reality. Of course, if you only have a hammer, everything looks like a nail ~ but everything is not a nail, and you don't only have a hammer. So you can think of these suttas as like Homebrew, which I recently learnt is a package of digital tools that can be downloaded to complement the existing tools on your computer. Except reality is not on your computer ~ at least I don't think it is, but who knows 🤷

Steps 1, 2 and 4 are straightforward enough, but I'm right there with you if your brain's letterbox raised a little red flag around 3 and 5: a whole and significant [[01. ## The Three Poisons
*greed, anger, and delusion*

The main cause of suffering is _tanha_ ( #craving, desire), which is easy enough to understand: we suffer when we want something we don’t have, and this is often referred to in the suttas: \[separation from loved ones, etc.] But it was something of a breakthrough for me (maybe I was a bit slow on the uptake) that \[_Pali_] ( #aversion) is the flip-side of [[clinging]]. 

Where it is obvious how desire causes suffering, it was less obvious to me that aversion is the desire for something to not be, although it is classically stated alongside the above separation, that proximity with unpleasant people and things and situations is equally insufferable.

That this had to dawn on me is a good illustration of _avijjya_ ( #ignorance) being the other cause of suffering: due to a fundamental misunderstanding about [[understand the true nature of reality|the nature of reality]], we mistake something to be a cause of happiness, when actually it is a cause of suffering, a categorical error the Buddha illustrated by referring to the poisoned-honey knife.

So there are said to be three causes of suffering, the Three Poisons of craving, aversion, and ignorance. But craving and aversion are essentially the same thing, and they both spring from ignorance – so the cure for suffering is wisdom, so that we don’t keep getting caught making the same mistake over and over and over again.
### The Defilements
#kilesa #defilements #wholesome #unwholesome #kusala #akusala #suffering 

The defilements are an interesting term, sometimes thrown around as jargon. Sometimes the term is used synonymously with the Three Poisons, and other times these three are taken to be the root of a more-granular list of defilements called *==kilesa==*. Either way, they are unwholesome states and events, and because they are unwholesome they cause suffering. 

In the [Vatthupama Sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.007.nypo.html), there are 16 defilements listed: 

1. *abhijjha-visama-lobha*, covetousness and unrighteous greed
2. *byapada*, ill will
3. *kodha*, anger
4. *upanaha*, hostility or malice
5. *makkha*, denigration or detraction; contempt
6. *palasa*, domineering or presumption
7. *issa*, envy
8. *macchariya*, jealousy, or avarice; selfishness
9. *maya*, hypocrisy or deceit
10. *satheyya*, fraud
11. *thambha*, obstinacy, obduracy
12. *sarambha*, presumption or rivalry; impetuosity
13. *mana*, conceit
14. *atimana*, arrogance, haughtiness
15. *mada*, vanity or pride
16. *pamada*, negligence or heedlessness; in social behavior, this leads to lack of consideration.
## Sense Restraint
*indriya samvara*

Buddha taught that we should restrain our senses, guard the sense gates ~ or doors, as a means of taming oneself. It could be said that everything he taught was about taming.      but also it is said that everything he taught is about dependent origination, about [[suffering and the end of suffering]] %%port the copy over from Jewel%%, and I can get behind it on the grounds of its logic but also from [[empiricism 🎯|experience]] 🏹, on empirical grounds. I had an experience confirmed with a teacher recently by learning that the profound calm and joyful interest in the breath that results from solid #concentration in #meditation is essentially the definition of *niramisa sukha*, meaning 'pleasure from beyond the senses' or, spookily, 'otherworldy pleasure'. It is the one #pleasure Buddha said would not cause suffering ~ he actively encourages us to delight in it, as a wholesome pleasure that draws one away from unwholesome pleasure derived from the sense realm. So it's real, and deserves the prominence it gets in the teachings. 

The result is one thing, but the skill of applying it was elusive for me ~ it and required a certain amount of conceptual scaffolding before I could take hold of it. More on that another time. It will do for now that *indriya samvara* sits on a par with #saddha as indispensable, and reasonably defensible: guarding the sense doors allows the mind to quieten and concentrate, even on something as boring as the breath; and as this quiet deepens, the #conditions arise for #insight.

A teaching couldn't be more fundamental than this, so its a very important idea and practice to understand, not misunderstand. 

Thero continues describing the practice, that by allowing an arisen experience to *be*, attention of the background/periphery manifests #indirectly ==\[and is diffuse?]==, like falling asleep happens indirectly when one directs the mind away from entanglement with mind, toward an anchor in the body or its shifting sensations. Such an inhabitance is alluded to by Frankl as well, in his idea of the space between stimulus and response. 

There is a trend among contemporary meditation circles to teach one-pointed focus on the foreground object, but this practice precludes awareness of the background, of the origin of experience.

It has also been important for me to recognise it is not quite true that one can be Buddhist without holding certain beliefs, because right view includes belief in rebirth, at least, and also kamma %%and%% ... Coming to terms with this has been helpful, because I think I was experiencing a generalised reluctance to take things on faith because I had thought I wouldn't need to. 

A great appeal of Buddhism for many Westerners is that one doesn't have to take on beliefs they cannot prove before they can start practising Buddhism, which I think is related to our [[fear of credulity]]. A whole Secular Buddhism movement has developed around this, and I could relate to it for a while because I didn't like the idea of *just accepting* beliefs I couldn't understand or prove to be justified. But I have since learnt that Buddhism requires a different kind of faith, which is more like a confidence in these beliefs, lightly held while one puts those beliefs to the test - so we *can* justify them, and Buddha never asked us to accept them without endeavouring to do so. 

It may be argued that rebirth can be interpreted psychologically/existentially and doesn't require cosmological belief in literal realms, but I wonder how that would impact one's practice: whether it would undermine effort or faith because one would believe they are not coming back for another life and/or that we escape from samsara upon death. If our consequences end with death, there is little motivation for ethical behaviour beyond how it supports a happy current life - the possibility of eternal discontent at least, and possibly rebirth in lower realms, becomes an incentive according to [[Pascal’s Wager]]: if there is such a possibility, the best bet is to assume that possibility - if it turns out there isn't an afterlife, the least you get is a wholesome current life.

Personally this reasoning is sufficient for me to assume rebirth is real, and other aspects of Buddhist cosmology make more sense to me than the Big Ban one-universe theory. These are not part of skilful view, and are discussed elsewhere including the [[#Metaphysics]] section below.

# What the Buddha Didn't Teach
## Psychotherapy
It has been helpful for me to learn that even *becoming an arahant doesn't operate on the level of the #personality* (Bodhi, p. 16), because meeting purported arahants whose behaviour was uninspiring to say the least, would sometimes leave me doubting the sangha *and* the dhamma: *If these are the results, I'm not interested.* 

This is relevant to faith because the fact he didn't teach psychotherapy doesn't undermine/misrepresent the efficacy of the dhamma and just means it is worthwhile complementing one's dhamma practice with things like shadow work.

Although one may become more compassionate, gentle, etc. because of the training, it is the training that causes this not the awakening itself, and an arahant may not necessarily iron out all the habits we may see as character flaws, like gruffness or aloofness or low emotional intelligence/awareness. This doesn't mean the dhamma isn't working, just that the dhamma operates on a level other than the personality. 

Arguing against the view that we can discount cosmological facets such like rebirth as "religious trappings of an ancient culture utterly inappropriate for the Dhamma of our technological age", Bhikkhu Bodhi writes in his short piece "[Dhamma Without Rebirth](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/bps-essay_06.html)" that when we

> downplay the doctrine of rebirth and explain the entire import of the Dhamma as the amelioration of mental suffering through enhanced self-awareness \[we] deprive the Dhamma of those wider perspectives from which it derives its full breadth and profundity. By doing so one seriously risks reducing it in the end to little more than a sophisticated ancient system of humanistic psychotherapy.

"Our motivation acquires the requisite maturity by the cultivation of right view, the first factor of the Noble Eightfold Path, which as explained by the Buddha includes an understanding of the principles of kamma and rebirth as fundamental to the structure of our existence."[^4]

## Metaphysics

> [!NOTE] Scope
> Buddhist practice may not lead us to insight into the nature of all reality, but it leads us to direct insight into the nature of the mind - which might be the same thing. These limitations impact faith for me, because if I want certain answers that Buddhism doesn't address then I need to either abandon those questions or complement my practice with other modalities of esoteric inquiry/investigation, such as shadow work.

Reading Harris helped me understand more clearly what I already suspected, which is that not all spiritual practices 'point to the same underlying reality', and the 'esoteric doctrines found within every religious tradition are not all derived from the same insights'. I would add that neither does science - for all its powers in certain areas, there is no confirmed theory about the nature of reality. %%Is there?%% So it's not like I need to abandon science altogether - it just happens to be limited in its scope, and I need to adjust my expectations, the same as I need to adjust my expectations of Buddhism, knowing that all of these traditions are limited in what they investigate. 

One benefit of spiritual practice though, is that where science relies on abstraction[^6] to describe reality, spiritual practice (or 'psychological technologies') gives the individual direct access to certain aspects of reality that is more empirical than the abstractions of science. Although the teachings generally rely on abstractions, their intention is to catalyse the insights of spiritual practice that can be experienced directly by any individual who puts in the right effort - ==this is what Buddha meant by 'seen individually the wise'.== Harris writes (20) about how individuals 'of all faiths have glimpsed many of the same truths', and that 'any view of consciousness and the cosmos that is available to the human mind can, in principle, be appreciated by anyone'. ==The suttas report something similar.==
- [ ] find where it says in the suttas that anyone with the five faculties and the six senses has the same potential to become enlightened, and %% there may be good examples in William James' Varieties of Religious Experience%%

Anyone having such an experience would find it difficult to articulate their findings to another, but they are individually transformed, and their alternative way of being inspires others to enquire about how they might apply the same teachings that lead to those findings and a similar transformation. %%What is the word in science when findings can be repeatedly reproduced?%% 

Scientific method is not easy to teach, and most of us don't have access to the sort of technology required to enter the game. The insights or conclusions vary depending on which abstraction the current scientist relied on. And the scope is limited, partly by ideology and partly by technology, but mostly by the simple truth that not everything can be observed at once - we can never know the whole directly, and that is not a limit of metaphysics, just of reality itself. The classic Zen garden gracefully refers to this. 

So neither science nor Buddhism or any other spiritual practice leads to an understanding of ultimate reality - that is my current position. But what Buddhism does offer, as [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] writes (20), is a 'truly sophisticated, empirical approach to understanding the human mind'. In Buddhism we have the idea that the whole world is (within) the fathom-long body and the mind is the forerunner of all states: if so, then understanding the mind equals understanding all reality? 

I really need to explore this more - get my head around it, so to speak. I would confidently say at least, that such understanding of the mind was the explicit limit of Buddha's scope: he taught an understanding that would lead to liberation from mind-made suffering; he didn't speak about other aspects of metaphysics because they didn't serve this goal - whether he knew about those aspects or not is irrelevant. 

All that's relevant is whether Buddhism addresses the concerns I have or ~solves the problems~ I think we need to solve. This is where my faith is sometimes impacted, because for example, Buddhism doesn't promise to operate on the level of the personality - as I said above, meeting purported arahants whose behaviour was uninspiring to say the least, would sometimes leave me doubting the sangha *and* the dhamma: *If these are the results, I'm not interested.* 

That he didn't teach psychotherapy doesn't undermine or misrepresent the efficacy of the dhamma - it just means  I need to complement my practice with modalities that do take a healthy self as their end goal, because I believe such a [[healthy sense of self]] is required for transcendence.

If I find out that Buddhism also doesn't address metaphysical questions I think are important, that is less a limitation of Buddhism than a limitation of my own capacity to curb my curiosity. 

Circling back to the spiritual experiences that anyone can have, which Harris describes as *psychological* experiences and Buddha described as ==\[Pali]==, and the fact that such experiences can be replicated - it is my hope, from this, that we limited humans will continue developing a form of something like transpersonal and experiential psychology that will help us transcend the differences of interpretation that result from divergent metaphysical worldviews. 

Any individual can have these experiences with appropriate training and guidance, and I suppose that dualistic or monistic beliefs become irrelevant in the direct experience of our shared ground. [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] (21) discredits Aldous Huxley's Leibnizian perennial philosophy on the grounds that Abrahamic religion is dualistic while Eastern traditions transcend duality, which is not enough to convince me that there isn't common ground in some form of universal experience - what Huxley called the 'highest common factor'.

But meanwhile I need to get my own house in order, which includes limiting myself to those areas of metaphysics that make the difference between suffering and flourishing.
