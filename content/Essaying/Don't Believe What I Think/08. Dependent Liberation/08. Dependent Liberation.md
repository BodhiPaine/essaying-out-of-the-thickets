An important aspect of skilful view is an understanding of dependent origination, also called dependent co-arising, the Buddha's teaching on how suffering is created and perpetuated - on the flipside we have [[#Dependent Liberation|dependent liberation]], which the teaching on how suffering ceases. 
# Dependent Origination

Also called the 12 Links of Suffering, dependent origination is most-often described as a process of twelve events, you could say, but they do not happen in a linear fashion ~ it is more like [[#Causality & Conditionality|they are simultaneously happening, or so I have heard]]. Despite them not happening in sequence, as such, we can interrupt the process at various points, thereby interrupting the recurring cycle of samsara, suffering. The twelve links are: 

1. Ignorance (*avijja*)
2. Kamma formations (*sankhara*)
3. Consciousness (*viññana*)
4. Mentality-materiality (*namarupa*)
5. Sixfold sense base (*salayatana*)
6. Contact (*phassa*)
7. Feeling (*vedana*)
8. Craving (*tanha*)
9. Clinging (*upadana*)
10. Existence (*bhava*)
11. Birth (*jati*)
12. Suffering (*dukkha*)

When I look at this list, I kind of go cross-eyed, and I have tried researching this stuff a bunch of times but it doesn't seem to stick. There is a process I learnt about recently that is at the heart of learning how to apply Buddhism, and it is a process described by the Pali terms *pariyatti*, *paṭipatti* and *paṭivedha*, meaning "learning", "practising" and "realising". By producing [[00. ## Causality & Conditionality
I am not yet sure whether these are essentially synonymous, but I do understand that causality in frameworks like dependent origination and the [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#the Eightfold Path|Noble Eightfold Path]] are understood to be #not-linear, but more like they are happening simultaneously. It may not even be as straightforward as that, and even though I think that questions about the mechanics of karma are one of the things the Buddha declared to be [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#The Ten Unprofitable Questions|unprofitable]], I still think it's useful to understand just how not-linear and even perhaps how not-understandable these mechanics are. 

Then we can focus on where we do have #agency instead of focusing on the academics of how are agency is interacting with reality - see the [[simile of the poisoned arrow]]. The following delightfully floral description from Bhikkhu Bodhi may be a good place to start: 

> quite different from the pattern of blind efficient causality which involves the incidental emergence of an effect out of its causal matrix, as for example when a series of geological changes triggers off an earthquake or a number of atoms combine to form some new molecule. The **relationship between the path and the goal belongs to a more complex order of causality**, one which can perhaps be pictured as a set of prior causes giving rise to an effect but can never be adequately and correctly comprehended in terms of this model. What we have here is not an instance of simple, one-directional causality proceeding forward unmodified in a straight line; we have, rather, a **species of teleological causality involving purpose, intelligence, and planned striving simultaneously projected towards and refracted from the aimed at effect in a process of reciprocal determination**. In the workings of this relationship not only does the path facilitate the achievement of the goal, but **the goal as well**, already present from the outset as the envisaged aim of striving, **itself bends back to participate in the shaping of the path**. Starting from man's awareness of the painful inadequacies of his existence, and his intuitive groping towards a condition where these are allayed, the formula proceeds to trace back, in terms derivative from and constantly checked against the goal, **the series of alterations he must induce in his cognitive and emotive makeup** to bring the goal into his reach.

What he seems to be saying here is that the interactivity of the parts comprising frameworks of understanding and behaviour like dependent origination and the Noble Eightfold Path is characterised by a *plurality of causes* resulting in a *plurality of effects* that do not necessarily lead from one to the other, but which are nonetheless related in ways that don't necessarily need to be comprehended for us to find #agency, and that agency is found in conducting that *series of alterations*.

It is like the [[simile of the poisoned arrow]]: when we are struck by a poisoned arrow, we don't need to know who shot it, what the arrow is made from, which angle it came from, etcetera, before we should remove the arrow; when we suffer at all, we don't need to know the minutiae of the causes and effects ~ we only need to know how to introduce the causes for non-suffering into our experience. The causes for non-suffering are the uprooting of the defilements: ignorance, greed and aversion. One way of achieving this is the [[#Dependent Liberation|dependent liberation]] described below, and in the [[SN 12.23 The Upanisa Sutta, 'Proximate Cause'|Upanisa Sutta]].

The Upanisa Sutta is the "Discourse on Supporting Conditions" and, whereas dependent origination describes the conditions that support suffering, transcendental dependent origination describes the conditions that support transcension of suffering.

==Than== says, “the teaching of dependent co-arising gives a detailed treatment of the first three noble truths, listing the sequence of conditions that lead to suffering”. The more I research and contemplate *saddhā* ('faith' in Buddhism), the more I understand that an exposition on faith is also, functionally and fundamentally, an introduction to Buddhism, which necessarily starts as an introduction to the Buddha himself, and also his dhamma and the sangha he founded 2600 years ago. So I am seeing that this essay is, or needs to be, [[a guide to becoming a disciple of the Buddha|a guide]] to becoming a disciple of the Buddha. I hadn't really considered this to be possible until I migrated to the Theravada tradition, and now it is no longer optional, but rather, optimal compared with remaining dependent on dhamma teachers, ordained or otherwise. Because faith in Buddhism starts with *saddhā* in the Triple Gem, in the Buddha, the dhamma, and the sangha. I'll unpack that a bit more later. 

Meanwhile it is important to appreciate, as most (reasonable and confident) people do, that faith in Buddhism doesn't start with faith in gurus, and it doesn't start with faith in God, or in anything [[supernatural]]. It does, however, tend to start with the end of faith in other things we once held dear: hedonism, the government, [[metaphysical materialism]], literalism, science, technology. People, especially Westerners, come to Buddhism because we are suffering and nothing in our culture seems to have an authentic solution to that problem. We try seeking pleasure because we think it is the opposite of suffering, and therefore a pathway to happiness. Fail. When that doesn't work, we blame the government ~ we try to [[the Fourth Estate|keep them accountable on behalf of the people]], but fail. When that doesn't work, we start to wonder if there might be something broken in the fabric: the #human-condition is a curse and *we're all going to hell in a hand basket*. If one doesn't murder themselves at this point, it is because they ~~are fortunate enough~~ have the #kamma and the conditions for it to ripen in the form of beginning to suspect there is something more to life, something we are missing and if we can just find it, the magic, the joy, the peace, the wonder, and the awe. 

Until then it all just feels like a massive drag, at least for many of us ~ I did meet one Australian monk recently who said he had a pretty chill kind of life, no major suffering, just a deep sense that there was something else to do here. The call to ordain dropped on him suddenly, and he cried tears of relief. 

That feeling of relief is the voice of *saddhā*, of a deeply felt[^2] conviction that there is a way to be genuinely happy in this world, in this life ~ and maybe, just maybe, if we can drop our attachment to the conditioning we have been steeped in for [[the post-Enlightenment era|the last 400 years or so]] ... since, roughly, Descartes, perhaps, although I don't know why I always feel the tendency to blame him, specifically. There is a [[where in history the West started to go so horridly astray|whole massive branch]] to be explored on the subject of ==where in history the West started to go so horridly astray==, and I'll get to that, don't you worry. In the midst of the despair that ensues in anyone who is only half-asleep, one eye perceiving the world as a quagmire of heinous bullshit most of the time, and the other clogged with conjunctivitis because even pollen is toxic now[^1], it really does come as a massive relief to come across the #dhamma. Finally, a community of people, thousands of years old and totalling ~5 billion, %%after waiting minutes for Claude to compile a complex response%% who have been wondering the same thing only wait ... that means this is not a modern problem after all. That right there alone is the sort of [[panoramic perspective|perspective]] that can pull a person out of the funk. 

This, I suppose, is why Buddha taught how suffering can be leveraged for the end of suffering ~ a paradox, I know, but paradoxes are just so damn cool, like a battle axe to the head of #literalism and #dualism and that devil of [[epistemology]]: #certainty.  He taught this in a discourse called the [Upanisa Sutta](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.23/en/sujato), and Bhikkhu Bodhi wrote [a brilliant essay about it](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/wheel277.html) called 'Transcendental Dependent Arising: A Translation and Exposition of the Upanisa Sutta'. Dependent arising (or dependent origination) is a chain of causality the Buddha discovered that explains why we suffer ~ transcendental dependent arising then (or dependent liberation) is a *set of instructions* for *how to transcend suffering*.

This is the sutta that first really brought me over to the idea that I had better get my faith on, and for that reason it gets a seat at the [[Essential Suttas#SN 12.23 The Upanisa Sutta, 'Proximate Cause'|Essential Suttas]] table.
