To doubt is to waver in opinion or judgment; to be 
in uncertainty as to belief respecting anything; to hesitate in belief; to be
undecided as to the truth of the negative or the affirmative proposition; to be undetermined. %%retain formatting of dangling 'to be', which is referenced below%%

What is doubt? Excellent question! First, doubt is one of [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances|the Five Hindrances]] to concentration in the practice of Buddhist meditation. But before we get to the first thing as it relates to applied Buddhism, a detour into [[epistemology]], my favourite! My initial formatting in the above definition inadvertently revealed something significant ~ *to be, is to doubt*. 

In epistemology %%(I would feel confident in saying)%% doubt is a prerequisite for knowledge ~ or, perhaps, for *true* knowledge. One might believe they know something, and have no doubt ~ this would be a nightmare, entrenched certainty that cannot be modified because there is no room for new evidence to be considered. 

Without doubt, no one would ever question what they *think* is true, but perhaps or probably isn't. So we can believe something to be true, without a doubt, and be completely wrong because doubt is not there as a check-and-balance. When doubt is present we can feel more confident that our knowledge is at least moving toward [[justified true belief]], even if we aren't certain about something we believe to be true.

So, doubt is a good thing. Don't be worried if you are in doubt ~ it just means that you are open to being wrong, to encountering new evidence that might displace your current belief. You're not alone in this, and this aspect of the #human-condition even has a name, 'epistemic self-doubt'[^3]. In fact if you are one of the few who are aware of their own epistemic self-doubt, you are in good company. Roush[^2] explains on *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* how Socrates perfected this. He was famously confident that he didn't know, and would 'go around town asking others for their answers and making awkward observations about their replies'. When he was infamously prosecuted and murdered for such brazen acknowledgement of the [[ignorance]] we all share, he told his prosecuters that his self-proclaimed ignorance 'had the salutary effect of making it possible for him to find out' because 'if he were sure that he already knew, then he would not have motivation to look for the answer'. 

So I didn't just make up all that stuff above, and if you are reading this then we are both part of an long and esteemed tradition of not-knowing. I feel grateful that somehow I have always been intuitively aware of this, and have found myself saying on occasion that 'ignorance is the gateway to knowledge'. I wasn't always grateful for this, because it made me awkward company pretty much anywhere I went in the suburbs of Adelaide at the time.

Descartes, on the other hand, famously declared, "I think, therefore I am." Two truth claims there: it is 'I' who thinks; and 'I' am. Neither of these claims hold water under the rubric of [[epistemology#Buddhist Epistemology|Buddhist epistemology]] as I understand it. According to my current understanding of [[epistemology#Cartesian epistemology|Cartesian epistemology]], Descarte said *cogito ergo sum* because the only thing he could be certain of is that he was thinking ~ therefore, his beingness was embedded in thinking, could not be separated from thinking. Buddhism tells a different story, thankfully: if I identified with every fleeting and inane thought that came to mind, I would be a blubbering mess; indeed, I *was* a blubbering mess for quite a long time as an adolescent and young adult, precisely because I hadn't yet learnt that great maxim of self-help and truth-seekers the world over: *You are not your thoughts.* So there, #Descartes was wrong. Phew! 

So far, in answer to our question, 'What is (the nature of) doubt?', we have at least some understanding: doubt is good, not necessarily a problem; the presence of doubt helps us to move closer to truth. Considering that, let's pivot back to [[#Doubt in the Five Hindrances | doubt in the Five Hindrances]].

### Buddhist Cosmology
[[Gethin]] says (115) the 'earliest strata' of texts (the Nikāyas/Agamas) do not contain a systematic cosmology, but details that were 'systematised into a coherent whole' in the abhidhamma[^7]. There are two abhidhamma streams: Theravādin; Sarvāstivādin (Chinese, Tibetan); but they share essentially the same cosmology. 

I wonder sometimes, *Are the sutta descriptions of the cosmos believed to be based on the Buddha's direct experience of such cosmological features as expanding and collapsing world-systems[^6], etcetera?* (He attained [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#the Three Knowledges|the Three Knowledges]], I know that much is reported in the suttas.) #buddhānusati Because such direct/empirical observation says a lot about the untapped [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#Developing the Mind|potential of the human mind]], even if such potential is available only to a buddha and there can be only one at a time in a world-system. 

If it's true that only a buddha experiences the Three Knowledges ... wait no, there are numerous accounts in the Theragata of arahants attaining the Three Knowledges. Whether they are necessary for enlightenment is probably the more-pertinent question. That they are possible is a good [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#Contemplation of the Triple Gem|contemplation]] for both *buddhānusati* and *dhammānusati* - for the Buddha who realised these knowledges, and for the dhamma he taught for us to realise them too.

## Limitations of the Dhamma
It might sound sacrilegious to say the dhamma has limits, but the Buddha declared repeatedly that his whole project was the cessation of suffering - nothing more, nothing less. Breaking down what this means is going to leave us with a clearer understanding of what we can and can't achieve in dhamma practice. It might be like sculpting, where we chip away beliefs that are not incorrect about Buddhism, leaving us with a small pile of wishes that Buddhism cannot grant, such as rebirth in an eternal afterlife or even [[07. What the Buddha Taught#Psychotherapy|personality health]] in this lifetime.

Not knowing the boundaries of Buddhism's scope, I sometimes feel doubt from the question of whether Buddhism is [[#Is Buddhism Holistic?|sufficiently holistic]] for the goals I would like to achieve.
### Is Buddhism Holistic?

> [!NOTE] Is Buddhism holistic?
> What if the scope of some other spiritual tradition is more encompassing of a truth more whole and complete than the Buddha’s truths of suffering and its cessation? Is there more to the self than Buddha understood? Does practising dhamma work at the level of personality, and if not, which modalities do I need to complement the practice?

The New Age movements and other movements around personal- or *self*-development have left a big imprint on the modern Western mindset, as well as having grown out of an existing self-obsession, meaning many of us have been conditioned to believe that wellbeing results from 'finding oneself' or from 'becoming one's best self’ and even, going back to the dawn of much Western thought, from the Delphic oracle’s injunction to 'know thyself', which rings a bit true if read to mean, 'know thy self is an illusion'. A real illusion, but an illusion nontheless. 

It’s easy to poke holes in most of the New Age stuff, but they do present a shiny facade that promises a kind of #wholeness that has a lot of appeal - many of us are fragmented from trauma, and chronically distracted to avoid the pain of not being whole. Buddhism doesn’t seem to have the same focus on becoming whole, or at one with the unconscious, although it does get close here. It doesn't seem to be 'integrative'. So it comes to mind occasionally to wonder, *Is Buddhism holistic?* *What if the scope of some other spiritual tradition is more encompassing, more whole and complete than the Buddha’s truths of suffering and its cessation?* It may be that I need to complement my Buddhist practice with other modalities. To know what I need to complement, I need to know [[07. What the Buddha Taught|where Buddhism starts and stops]].

## Epistemic Self-doubt

## I Should be 'Helping'
Related to the common mythperception that [[#Self-awakening is Selfish]], a common source of doubt that undermines one's willingness to apply effort in meditation is the belief that one should be helping in ways that are more direct, not in ways reliant upon obscure [[07. What the Buddha Taught#Metaphysics|metaphysical mechanisms]] one might never understand (cf. [[AN 10.95, Uttiya Sutta, ‘With Uttiya’|AN 10.95]], Uttiya Sutta). 

This view can be challenged a number of ways: by recourse to ==Buddha's teaching that self-awakening is the superior way to help oneself and all beings==%%cite%%; and by recognising that the desire to help is not always wholesome, can sometimes be conceited and deluded - may even be an expression of codependency or spiritual bypassing: *I need to save the world, no time for my own practice!*

The mechanics of how one's practice influences change in society gets its own guernsey in the chapter on What the Buddha Taught, [[07.02. Can Dhamma Save the World?|'Can Dhamma Save the World'?]] By modelling an alternative way of being?, or by somehow influencing the collective consciousness? or both. Is one of these ways more or less direct than the other? Do we need to know *how* it works, or is it enough to have *saddhā* that our mental training is [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#For the Welfare and Happiness of Many, Gods and Humans|for the benefit of gods and humans]]. Asking how it works might be akin to asking who shot [[simile of the poisoned arrow|the poisoned arrow]].

In AN 10.95 again, the Buddha talks needing to *save ourselves from the world*, not *save the world*. 

And the desire to serve something greater than myself can be a genuine sign of having self-actualised #self-actualisation; or it could be a manifestation of #codependency, which is a problem akin to [[spiritual-bypassing]] . And the desire to serve something greater than myself: is this a sign of having [[self-actualised]]; or a manifestation of codependecy? IF it is codependency THEN this supports my move to prioritise self-awakening because codependency never served anyone. IF it is self-actualisation, only good can come from serving the transcendent.

A less-extreme position is simply that like all desire, a desire to save the world[^9] is conditioned - my upbringing has left me with the impression that my worth is dependent on 'helping', on *doing something* about the situation we share. I believe this tips over into codependency because identification is at play, *I am the sort of guy who is* doing something *to help.* 

Considering this helps me to let go of proliferation about ways I could be helping more directly (proliferation that causes doubt about the value of ==bhavana==) and gives me permission to get my own house in order before prioritising 'helping' others. %%something about this should be mentioned in [[06. Cultivating Saddhā]]%%

At its worst though, such proliferation could be precisely what holds me back from any progress, let alone awakening, because codependency is the need to be needed: I need to help; if I escape samsara, what about the beings left behind? %%Mara's 'holiness spiral'%% %%which reminds me I have hardly even dealt with the whole bodhisattva vs arahant thing, the Mahayana conditioning%%

#### The Ripple Effect
One of the first and most-common arguments I hear in support of dedicating oneself to something that otherwise might be perceived as quite selfish is [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#The Ripple Effect|the ripple effect]]: we model an alternative to the neurotic and fevered pursuit of happiness through pleasure, and lead by example. 
### Buddhism Doesn't Require Beliefs
When I say that out loud it sounds ridiculous, because any activity requires belief. We wouldn't do anything if we didn't believe in anything. Nonetheless I think this myth makes it hard for some Westerners to start believing things when they get a little deeper into the practice. 

It has certainly been important for me to recognise and accept it is not quite true that one can be Buddhist without holding certain beliefs, because holding right view requires belief in ==rebirth, at least, and also kamma %%and%%== ... Coming to terms with this has been helpful, because I think I was experiencing a generalised reluctance to take things on faith because I had thought I wouldn't need to. 

A great appeal of Buddhism for many Westerners is that one doesn't have to take on beliefs they cannot prove before they can start practising Buddhism, which I think is related to our [[fear of credulity]]. A whole Secular Buddhism movement has developed around this, and I could relate to it for a while because I didn't like the idea of *just accepting* beliefs I couldn't understand or prove to be justified. 

But I have since learnt that Buddhism requires a different kind of faith, which is more like a confidence in these beliefs, lightly held while one puts those beliefs to the test - so we *can* justify them, and Buddha never asked us to accept them without endeavouring to do so.
