Something that has become increasingly clear to me in my *saddhā* research is that a major sticking point in the cultivation of Buddhist faith is the worldview we Westerners have inherited mostly unconsciously. So I started researching worldviews themselves, and have come to recognise that any cosmology/worldview I might hold is a choice, 

conscious or unconscious — even the Big Bang and evolution are only hypotheses that have become accepted as scientism dogma (Sheldrake, Harpur). Whereas my worldview has been previously inherited without much question, I can make a conscious choice to adopt a Buddhist cosmology, knowing that the beliefs therein at least promote wholesome behaviour, even if they are not strictly "true" descriptions of something we can verify. Such a position is supported by [[Pascal’s Wager]], and is the moral argument for and from a Buddhist worldview that has been discussed by Bodhi as well as Dostyoevsky, called [LM]

By making this conscious choice I empower my own agency to interpret my experience of the world in a way that makes sense to me. It doesn't make sense to me that once there was nothing, and then there was something - the [[one-free-miracle model]] of the cosmos. It also doesn't make sense to me that there was nothing (except for something, a god) and then there was something made by a being-thing without a cause. I don't believe there is no first cause - I believe everything existed always, and will continue existing always, just in varying forms and according to various trajectories of evolution. 

This may or may not be true, but either way it doesn't actually matter - I cannot know the whole of reality, only a portion, so I have no way to verify whether we live in an eternal multiverse or a finite universe. This is [[metaphysics]]. All that matters is how one worldview over the other impacts my conduct - I choose the worldview that most supports being a good and happy being.

For example, do I believe it is possible for a person to become [[#Is Awakening Possible?|awakened in this lifetime]]? Generally speaking, yes I do - however, this belief is a choice and I don't yet have any empirical evidence for it, so for now I take it on #faith, or *saddhā*, meaning I believe I have good reasons for believing awakening is possible in this lifetime. If I didn't believe this were possible, it would radically impact the amount and type of effort I apply in my practice.
# Beliefs
For the purpose of this essay through *saddhā* and to support my own Buddhist practice, I have been trying to understand both the 'dominant Western worldview' and my own metaphysical beliefs, to see where I sit on the map. Turns out (perhaps unsurprisingly) that the further I get into this, the more I understand there isn't one *dominant* worldview - more like a patchwork of beliefs that are stitched together to form something that might loosely be called metaphysical materialism or metaphysical realism. 

Considering there is no great single edifice I can step out from underneath, I thought it might be helpful to see which beliefs I share with certain expressions of modern science, because getting them out in the open may be a good way of seeing them for what they are. I am hoping this will help me to see where I don't have to worry about whether my worldview motivates practice or subtly and half-consciously undermines it. Just another angle I guess.
## Is Awakening Possible?
Do I believe it is possible for a person to become awakened in this lifetime? Generally speaking, yes I do - however, this belief is a choice and I don't yet have any empirical evidence for it, so for now I take it on #faith, or *saddhā*, meaning I believe I have good reasons for believing awakening is possible in this lifetime. If I didn't believe this were possible, it would radically impact the amount and type of effort I apply in my practice.

There are whole [[living traditions]] that prioritise scholarship and memorising the suttas because they believe we are too deep in the sasana to become arahants in this lifetime, so they make merit hoping this will cause fortunate rebirth, ideally at the time of Matreya. Pure Land practitioners chant the name of Amitabha in the conviction they will be reborn in the realm of Amitabha, and in Sucitto's pilgrimage account they stay at a small Japanese temple where the whole practice is chanting and banging a drum. There must be numerous other examples.

There are also whole living traditions that prioritise awakening over all else, including the Early Buddhist Theravadan tradition I am planning to ordain in.
## The Self
Is the self a permanent, bounded, independent, separate *thing*? I don't think so. I think it is a process, both real and illusory - a real illusion, in the sense a mirage is real, even if the oasis is not. 
## Objectivity
I don't believe only third-person facts/observations 'count' as evidence or as reality. Subjective experience is more effective for understanding what I need to understand: how to be happy. Even if I can't satisfy various metaphysical curiosities I have, as also science cannot. 
## Physical Reality
I don't believe only the physical is 'real,' as in scientific or metaphysical materialism. 
## Psychic Phenomena
I do believe in psychic phenomena such as telepathy, clairvoyance, prophetic dreaming, astral travel, etc., and I consider its study is not just parapsychology or pseudoscience, but genuine fringe science - once these phenomena are experienced directly, it won't be 'para-' psychology and their place in the scientific canon doesn't matter. Until then I also have no known good reason to disbelieve the various accounts, which also applies to my faith in rebirth.
## Rebirth

An essential aspect of [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#Skilful View|skilful view]], I do believe in rebirth, primarily because I came into this world with affinities I can't explain without reference to previous existence. I also have no known good reason to disbelieve the various accounts of rebirth.

In [[MN 60 Apaṇṇaka Sutta, 'A Safe Bet'|MN 60]], the Buddha claims, very explicitly, "Since there actually is another world, one who holds the view ‘there is no other world’ has wrong view." In lieu of having our own direct experience of this metaphysical truth claim, it is advised in MN 60 that a wise or skilful person would *assume* it to be true, just in case - if it isn't true and there is actually no other world, then at least one's #wholesome motivation yields the fruit of happiness and welfare in this lifetime. Such an argument seems to be essentially consistent with [[Pascal’s Wager]]. I find these grounds to be sufficient for choosing to believe in rebirth, so this aspect, at least, of skilful view, gets a green stamp for epistemic status. 
## History as Linear Progress
I do tend to believe history is linear and progressing to some idealised evolved state, and that I need to *stay and help*, but knowing the [[ripple effect]] helps assuage this. This view is something of a problem, because it tends to lead me down paths where I think about how 'raising consciousness' through becoming awakened contributes to a more-evolved society, which might just be wishful soteriological thinking. #soteriology
## Dualism
I don't believe in Cartesian dualism or the idea that humans are somehow separate from nature - the mind is certainly not separate from the body. We are all connected, somehow, as described at least by Jung's collective unconscious and perhaps by Buddha's idea of conditionality. 
## Consciousness
I don't believe consciousness ends at death: I believe 

- in rebirth
- and kamma, 
- and that appropriate attention somehow results in liberation.

I have questions about the nature of consciousness, its origins, etc., and what happens to it after death and arahantship, but  I try to remember that I don't need to know *how* it works, just that kamma somehow travels through consciousness, and I am [[03. Saddhā|confident]] that it does. I would do well to remember more often the simile of the poisoned arrow: asking where the arrow came from, etc., doesn't get the arrow removed, and you could die asking.

I can drive a car without knowing exactly how it works.

# The Psychological Interpretation
Popular among secular Buddhists, some schools of Zen %%at least one%%, and Tibetan Buddhism, the psychological interpretation of cosmology takes the realms and other features of Buddhist cosmology to metaphorically represent states of mind. The first three *bhumīs* 'apply to levels of consciousness as well as realms of existence'[^1], in a classic case of double-vision, or just that both of these are literal?, though one is psychological and the other cosmological. Punnadhammo includes an endmatter chapter about this interpretation, which was also debated by Bhikkhu Bodhi and %%see NotebookLM for the existentialist he debated with%%

I think there is considerable value in considering the psychological interpretation first even, because it may be easier for Westerners to relate to the dhamma more practically, easily, and immediately. This means beginning at the metaphorical level, and once I have access to that I can start leaning more into [[#The Literal Interpretation|the literal interpretation]]. Maybe I would call this entry-level Buddhism, where we get in on the ground floor. That certainly maps with a way of approaching the dhamma I learnt from my Zen teacher, which is that its practice operates on two levels: the #therapeutic and the #transpersonal. It might be that the psychological interpretation is therapeutic, and the literal is transpersonal. %%place a block anchor here because this is a central idea I don't have a place for in the outline yet.%%

One of the first times I learnt about this interpretation was from a Tibetan monk who said something like the experience of anger *is the hell realm* - one doesn't have to wait for death to be reborn in the hell realms. Every cranky person we meet is obviously already suffering, and that suffering doesn't depend on belief in a multiverse.

While the psychological interpretation might be a good entry point, 

it is quite a leap to go from there to a full-blown literal interpretation, but I am happy to lean-in to that because I find the [[panoramic perspective]] consoling I suppose. That doesn't sound very scientific, but it starts with the idea that our whole present is nested in causes and conditions that are largely out of our control ... that takes the pressure off, somehow; and if rebirth is real I have infinite chances - this one 'dress rehearsal' isn't my only shot. %%link this with Kundera%%
