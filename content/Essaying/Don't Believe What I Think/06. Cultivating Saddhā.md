#### Sammā Sambuddho, a Buddha perfected by himself
#wisdom 

The Buddha was self-awakened, not by following another's teaching, and truly a cosmological marvel[^2] because such a being appears only once the [[saddhamma, true dhamma]] has long vanished from the world. Khantipālo highlights that the Buddha was a regular human being who realised the truth by virtue of his own efforts: he was not bestowed special or arcane insight by a god, and 'owes his enlightenment to none'. The Buddha was not descended from some supernatural realm[^3] or being, and yet has transcended human limitations. We might not have the potential to become a buddha because that would defy the laws of cosmology, but we can become enlightened disciples, arahants, by adhering to the Buddha's instructions.

This is inspiring because we each have all the same six senses and [[04. ##### the Three Knowledges
==*tevijja*==
The Buddha famously refused to answer certain questions about #metaphysics, and in discourses like [MN 71](https://study.jhana.info/suttas/mn71) *Tevijja Vacchagotta Sutta*, 'on the Three Knowledges', explicitly denied that he was omniscient. #omniscience In that sutta he clarifies what knowledge he did gain during the three watches of the night he awakened:

- recollection of past lives
- the passing away and rebirth of beings according to their karma
- unconditioned liberation of nibbana, by eradication of the defilements

Technically these are called: 

- recollection of past abodes (*pubbenivāsanussati-ñāṇa*), 
	- which ==apparently== meant he could see past lives across countless cosmic cycles, including past worlds (*bhājana-loka*) and cosmic epochs
- the Divine Eye (*dibbacakkhu* / *cutūpapāta-ñāṇa*)**, 
	- meaning knowledge of the passing away and rebirth of sentient beings across the 31 planes of existence according to their karma
- destruction of the effluents (*āsavakkhaya-ñāṇa*), 
	- meaning penetrative, liberating insight that eradicates the defilements of craving (*taṇhā*), conceit (*māna*), and speculative views (*diṭṭhi*)

#### *Ekāyano Maggo*, The 'Direct Path'
It can be helpful in cultivating *saddhā* to consider the idea of *ekāyano maggo*, which translates as "direct path; a path leading toward (only) one destination", which is *nibbāna*, freedom from suffering. This does not mean there is only one path - every being walks a unique path - but just that *the* Path, the Eightfold Path, leads only in one direction - toward the cessation of suffering.
#### A Whole and Complete Path
There are some teachings and suttas that are considered to be internally sufficient as a practice leading to [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering 🎯| complete liberation]]. I first encountered this idea in Tibetan Buddhism, and later from my Zen teacher who referred to the Four Foundations of Mindfulness as a complete path. 

Ajahn Sumedho says in his book *The Four Noble Truths* [^6], "the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, the Buddha’s teaching on [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#The Four Noble Truths|the Four Noble Truths]], has been the main reference that I have used for my practice over the years", and "this one sutta contains all that is necessary for understanding dhamma and for enlightenment". Remember, the Four Noble Truths contain the Eightfold Noble Path as the Fourth Noble Truth, which is that *there is a path out of suffering*. 

A good one to contemplate if the ol' doubt has reared its head again.

## Heavenly Destiny
🗂️ #unsupportive

In Soma Thera’s essay, 'Faith In The Buddha’s Teaching', there are so many rich ideas in just the first four pages, and then more all the way through, starting with a guarantee the faithful will go to heaven:

- one who has mere/'but' faith in and affection for the Buddha and his teachings, when they are correctly expounded, is destined for heaven (MI 142[^1]), ... 

Leading with this does not give much support to [[#Canonical Support for *Saddhā*]] because our conduct is the only determinant of the future we might expect, and belief matters only to the extent it motivates [[wholesome conduct]] #wholesome #conduct. There is something questionable about saying one with mere faith is "as it were taken by the hand and placed in heaven", considering a belief exists in some Christians that merely inviting Jesus into one's heart ensures they have a place in heaven, freedom from sin, etc.

He does say elsewhere in the essay that genuine faith - or faith based on genuine understanding - necessarily motivates wholesome conduct because one of those ideas is #kamma. Still though, **the heaven guarantee troubles me** because doesn't Buddha say elsewhere that we cannot know where kamma will lead, beyond good=good? *If someone does a lot of unwholesome stuff in their life and gains genuine faith right at the end of their life, are they still going to heaven?* And apart from the actions in this life: what if past-life (bad) kamma is yet to find the conditions for ripening? Couldn't even the faithful then still be reborn in hell? Or can it be assumed that if one has gained human rebirth then all their bad past-life kamma must have been burned?

These (kinds of) questions honestly do need some kind of resolution for me and as such the [[#Heavenly Destiny]] teaching doesn't sit well with me in terms of reasoned conviction.

As the cook pays attention to his master's reactions, to know whether a practice is developing in the right fashion, a meditator must pay attention to the 'sign of the mind', and we do this by developing *[[07. It makes complete sense to me that I should have a range of techniques up my sleeve, so I guess I can stamp this with ✅ denoting a confident epistemic state. There is no good argument I know of that supports the teaching or application of a single sole technique or approach to meditation. 

Of course we need to not flit around between objects or meditation styles because *This one isn't working*, but after applying ourselves enough to one approach we need to check-in with whether it is working or not. Certainly the one-method end of the spectrum is generally unadvisable, it is safe to say.

A competent cook pays attention to whether his master is satisfied with the dishes being served. In the same way, a competent meditator is aware of whether his practice is improving #concentration and reducing [[The Defilements|defilements]], or not. # Understanding the Buddha's Teaching
It should go without saying that an important aspect of cultivating saddhā is understanding [[what the Buddha actually taught, and did not teach]], but this is easier said than done. I have dedicated a whole 'chapter' of the essay to this, and there are some preliminary comments worth making here and/or there. There is a belief among some people that "all religions are the same", or that all religious experience is aimed at the same goal, but even if there may be similarities between various experiences had by practitioners along the way, it was helpful for me to learn from Harris that the major religions' "truth claims about reality are mutually incompatible". So it is going to be important to understand, *What are the 'truth claims' of Buddhism?* 

Although I have some understanding of this already, there are certain areas of metaphysics and psychology that are not addressed by Buddhism, and sometimes I get stuck on these, thinking that Buddhism is not sufficient for what I want to achieve or contribute to achieving as humans in this weird experience of existing. So I need to draw some boundaries around where Buddhism starts and stops, otherwise my faith in Buddhism is compromised by something that isn't actually to do with Buddhism itself, but with my goals and aspirations. 

I can say with some confidence that, for example, I don't really need to know whether the universe has a beginning and an end or is actually an eternal multiverse, but I do need to give some thought to how I feel about rebirth. I am also becoming increasingly comfortable accepting that Buddhism may not be as holistic as I would like it to be - even the most-sincere dhamma practice may not do any work at the level of personality, but there are other complementary models of transpersonal psychotherapy that are sufficiently dhamma-aligned that I can incorporate them in my practice without ~betraying my allegiance~ to the Triple Gem or compromising the authenticity of my practice.

# Developing the Mind

Sometimes I fancy that developing the mind to its greatest potential is a good-enough reason to pursue this path, regardless of how I feel about whether my efforts directly benefit others or how I feel about realms as literal cosmological realities or metaphors for psychological states.

This is a relatively easy foothold for faith, but still sometimes I get jagged on how my inherited worldview influences even the extent to which I believe I can develop the mind. Our perception of the world is (somewhat pre-)determined by our beliefs and expectations, which means that someone who is still half-unconsciously immersed in the worldview of metaphysical materialism is likely to disbelieve that certain psychic phenomena are possible, [[including awakening]], whereas someone with a Buddhist (or shamanic, etc.) worldview would believe they are possible. I don't know where I get jagged exactly. 

I know I don't necessarily want or need psychic powers, but even to experience the jhanas in meditation I need to believe: 1) it is possible to achieve jhana; and 2) I have the capacity to achieve them. The jhanas were described by buddha as superior-human states, and they are certainly states of non-ordinary consciousness, characterised by *niramisa sukha*, 'pleasure from beyond the senses'. Being able to experience these states at all, let alone at will with the right training, was never a part of what my inherited worldview offered. It is another good-enough reason for me to defect from materialism and adopt a worldview that does offer ways of experiencing these states, replete with instructions that are, granted, a bit coded - but they are experiments that any one can repeat.

Of course this got me thinking about [[a well-developed mind|what actually constitutes a well-developed mind]], which surely begins with *not holding to fixed views*, which I have always appreciated.

# The Ripple Effect
One of the first and most-common arguments I hear in support of dedicating oneself to something that otherwise might be perceived as quite selfish is the ripple effect: we model an alternative to the neurotic and fevered pursuit of happiness through pleasure, and lead by example. 

It is not that I don't believe that such social impact is real - the ripple effect is undeniable, and gets a stamp ✅ - it's just that I have stupidly high expectations of myself and for a long time I was immersed in the bodhisattva ideal, meaning *it doesn't feel sufficient*, for me, that my impact on society comes through the minor and fleeting exchanges I have with fewer and fewer people as I get closer and closer to monasticism. 

Nonetheless, it's a reliable fallback because it's hard to doubt the efficacy of practice on this level.
