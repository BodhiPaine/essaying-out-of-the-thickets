Perhaps my first encounter with Buddhist faith (*saddhā*) was at a Tibetan centre in Adelaide, inquiring about how to hold right view when I had not yet had direct experience of the truth myself – and being told I needed to take it on faith. This didn’t exactly inspire ~confidence~ in me at the time, but there are seven other Path factors to consider and so I focused on those. In fact, I think that is part of the advice I got: focus on the other Path factors, and faith in right view will naturally develop, which I do understand in ways I hope to cover in this essay. 

However, a wheel with a single untended spoke is bound to yield a bumpy ride at best, and so it was: not looking into right view because I thought it could wait until later in my development, I left untended a wide range of half-(un)conscious doubts that would rear their head from time to time. And by ‘wide’ I mean ‘cosmological’: is enlightenment in this lifetime within the range of my worldview? If not, then doubt. If so, then striving. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

It may be more pertinent to first point out the elephant in the room: ‘faith’ is almost a dirty word in the minds of many secular folk, especially modern Westerners like myself, and especially because it is tinged with religiosity. No sciencey person wants their credibility to be tainted by being perceived as faithful, where 'faith' is taken to mean 'belief without evidence'. In dhamma practice, faith has a different function (more like 'conviction' or 'confidence'), as denoted by the Pali, *saddhā*. [[What the Buddha Taught|Rahula]] (8) has a nice way of putting it: 

> Almost all religions are built on faith—rather 'blind' faith it would seem. But in Buddhism emphasis is laid on 'seeing', knowing, understanding, and not on faith, or belief. In Buddhist texts there is a word *saddhā* (Skt. *sraddha*) which is usually translated as 'faith' or 'belief'. But saddha is not 'faith' as such, but rather 'confidence' born out of conviction.

Rahula says it is "'confidence' born out of conviction", and I know this is better than blind faith because it requires considering the evidence or claims or arguments to discern whether they are reasonable, but conviction is still a kind of faith. It is certainly a step short of knowledge or direct experience, but such faith is more like a reasoned decision to trust or believe the Buddha’s teachings, with awareness that I need to check the claims for myself, which of course the Buddha encouraged. Importantly, he didn’t encourage us to just appraise the claims conceptually, but said *ehi-passika*, 'come and see', not come and believe. Rather than demanding blind belief, the Path is more like starting with lightly held belief, and seeing where it takes you.

The Buddha never asked us to commit the sort of 'blind belief' that makes our local half-(un)conscious metaphysical materialist relatively okay with bleeding from the anus due to unknown causes, but never from the eyes. Forget about statues bleeding from the eyes! Just a hallucination. What the Buddha did ask of us ~ nay, demanded, sometimes with a stern admonishment ~ is that we hear the dhamma, reflect upon the dhamma, apply the dhamma, and see for oneself, *ehipassika*,%%what are the sutta references  for this?%% whether dhamma is true, or not.

Because we place trust in so many things without even realising it and the weather forecast is just the beginning, because, not yet feeling sufficiently righteous about [[epistemology|the right way to know]], we[^1] proceed to scoff at others for having faith in the so-called supernatural or ('Heaven forbid', we say, apparently unaware of the ~irony~) religion, only because our scientism hasn't yet discovered that the super- becomes redundant upon direct experience of something like prophetic dreams or spontaneous astral travel during a near-death experience or Jesus/Mohammad/Gandalf at the end of the tunnel or ... skies raining frogs.

'Seeing is believing,' we say, because we haven't been with Buddhism for long enough as a culture to understand that the mind itself is also a [[sense faculty]], and one that can 'see' things the eyes cannot. Considering the mind as a sense faculty in its own right though, requires a pivot: a sharp turn to avoid any quippyness about Bruce Willis telling bedtime stories and \[his charge] whispering, "I see dead people." 

Because the point here is just that faith doesn't have to be scary, anymore than ignorance has to be shameful: we've all got one (a mind), and I hope in this essay to deepen my own conviction, at least, that learning how to direct our attention with what I am now deciding to call 'skilful faith' might leaven our attachment to being afraid of feeling stupid for choosing to believe in something that might at least be liberative ~ considering we already do faith all, the, time, on the basis of many things we might never be able to know directly for ourselves. And in the Buddhist context, a human being ashamed of ignorance is like a fishing being ashamed of swimming.

Will the Earth get eaten by the sun, I don't know. Will the bus arrive on time so I can not feel like a numpty for arriving sweaty and dishevelled at my friend's place? 🤷 Is reality made of waves, or particles? Is there an afterlife? Who knows. Is history linear, or cyclical? Did something really come from nothing? What will I eat for dinner if I get home from work and the kids have eaten all the ice cream? 

Do you get what I'm saying? 

The assumptions we take for granted as the fundamentals of the science are just that, assumptions. Many of them can never be verified %%Correct?%%, because they are [[metaphysics|speculative]] (read: fictional[^6]). Some of the discoveries that have knocked old-paradigm physics off the throne are so weird they may as well be legit science fiction. 

And yet we place so much trust and faith in so many of the conclusions we arrive at from those fictions, without even realising it, and then we have the gaul to scoff at others for having faith in God without even asking, 'Hey so, what is it you actually believe in? I'm curious.' The modern religious faith I have encountered in others is often deeply metaphorical and literary, a kind of psychological mechanism that motivates [[morality|good conduct]], and has no actual relation to what the science zealots and militant atheists think they are scoffing at. It's crazy, the hubris of declaring that one knows what is false in the domains of human experience and culture we are talking about here.

And I say this because I know that hubris personally: I've done my own bit of scoffing at the 'Bible bashers' and the 'JoHos' and the good-vibes-only hippies[^2]. Worse, I have scoffed at myself for entertaining the idea of hell realms and devas and the efficacy of [[mettā meditation|imagining others being happy]]. And worst of all, the doubt that motivates all the scoffing is precisely what prevents us from accessing a way of being that might actually be liberative, which is a good place to pivot into [[Pascal’s Wager]]:  

choosing to consciously, deliberately, after due consideration, believe in a [[worldview]] that *might* be liberative is categorically better than continuing to unconsciously, flailingly, after considering mostly what kind of infinite pleasure one can extract from the only finite planet they can see with their eyes, believe in a worldview that is flagrantly *not* liberative but scuttling the very same planet.[^3]

%%I have drifted a long way from beginning to say what the point here is%%: that faith doesn't have to be scary ~ we do it all the time, except our pre-dominant faith is in materialist hedonism, which keeps us trapped, at best, in dopamine loops that make us cranky when we don't get our ice cream and, at worst, in *samsara*.

I think it has taken me a long time to write this because I was dancing around just saying it out loud, because I am worried it will trigger some science zealot into an angry (read: fearful) rebuttal: [[metaphysical materialism]] and post-Enlightenment rational literalism are a dead-end, having stripped the world of half its reality and left us dangling in a perception of spacetime arbitrated by the 'scientific priesthood', with nowhere to go but the grave, or an eternal afterlife that is not guaranteed to be pleasant. 

There is nothing liberative, that I know of, about the dominant worldview of modern Western cultures: if there were, we wouldn't be neurotically ferreting our way around the planet, eating everything we hope will displace, even just for a few moments, the discomfort of being unsure and afraid and [[human]]; the sort of rampant consumerism we have somehow normalised wouldn't get a foothold in a culture whose dominant worldview included a means of (a [[psychological technology]] for) training one's mind and heart to be liberated from suffering.

It's honestly weird, how I baulk at saying this out loud in a space that is currently private, but which is intended to eventually go public: I have solid faith in the human potential to [[Essential Suttas#SN 12.23 The Upanisa Sutta, 'Proximate Cause'|transcend suffering]], but phrases like 'transcend suffering' are so abstract and easy to misunderstand if one doesn't have a decent understanding of the Buddhist worldview that I really am wary of using them in case a militant atheist gets their knickers in a knot of such fierce defensiveness that they just switch off and stop reading. But then, as I consider it now, even the Buddha was reluctant to teach until a deva approached him and assured him there were enough people in the world with 'little dust in their eyes' that teaching the #dhamma the Buddha had realised would be worth a shot. So I really should stop baulking[^5], because there is genuinely good logic at play in what Buddha taught.

Now don't go thinking I have just compared myself to the Buddha. This essay is titled 'Don't Believe What I Think' for a reason: I do not claim to be making truth claims here. What I am doing is #thinking-in-public, in the spirit of [[essaying|Montaigne]]: I am trying out ideas that have been beneficial to me, most of which happen to be Buddhist ideas; I am just saying that I will try not to expect this essay will be firebombed by science bigots or [[realism]] trolls, and I am asking everyone else to accept the [[_Caveat]] I am stumbling around here. #caveat I do not claim to be an authority on Buddhism: I am a disciple of Buddha, who despite his extraordinary wisdom and [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] talent, was himself reluctant to say there is a way for humans to transcend suffering.

I see it now - thank you, #thinking-in-public . My hesitation is, itself, resulting from a crisis of faith. I do sincerely believe that the Buddha's #dhamma presents a robust [[psychological technology|psychospiritual methodology]] for training the mind to see reality clearly so that we don't repeatedly suffer the mistake of conflating the mirage with the oasis. Hedonism is failing us, clearly 👈🏼 *not* liberative. Eudaimonia is an upgrade, but still not the whole package: flourishing is a legitimate option, now that [[transpersonal psychology]] has hit the scene. But even the most cutting-edge new-paradigm psychological modalities of the West do not cut the mustard (seed) when compared with the potential that Buddhism offers. 

I know I am starting to sound like a zealot now, and I am okay with that in the same way science zealots are okay with being that: it's a matter of conviction, of faith, of *saddhā* (which has not been monopolised by Buddhism: *saddhā* just happens to be the same idea[^4] expressed in Pali). To articulate why I feel this conviction, I think it is time, now, to actually pivot ~ a full 180, to the other side of the coin: [[01. The Nature of Doubt]].
