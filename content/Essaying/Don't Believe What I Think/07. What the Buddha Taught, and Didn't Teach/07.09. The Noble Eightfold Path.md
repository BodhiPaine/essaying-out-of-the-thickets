*a set of non-linear instructions for conduct that moves one toward liberation* 

When Buddha realised the Four Noble Truths, that there is suffering, a cause of suffering, an end to the cause of suffering ... the fourth Noble Truth was that the Noble Eightfold Path is the end to the cause of suffering. The eight Path factors are 

1. Skillful[^1] View (*sammā-diṭṭhi*)
2. Skillful Aspiration (*sammā-sankappa*)
3. Skillful Speech (*sammā-vācā*)
4. Skillful Action (*sammā-kammanta*)
5. Skillful Livelihood (*samma-ājīva*)
6. Skillful Effort (*sammā-vayama*)
7. Skillful Mindfulness (*sammā-sati*)
8. Skillful Concentration (*sammā-samādhi*)
9. Skillful Consumerism

Okay, so that's 9 - and there are also two other Path factors that are experienced at fruition of the Eightfold Path, being Skilful Knowledge and Skilful Liberation. This is called the Noble Tenfold Path, and because I value the consideration of contemporary scholars that we need to include Skilful Consumerism at least in our own practice, if not as a doctrinal appendage, I have started rendering the Path as the nFold Noble Path.
# A Note on Translation
The Pali word *sammā* (Sanskrit, *śamyā*) translates as "right", "correct", "wholesome", "appropriate" or "skilful", where "wholesome" means any action that moves *toward* [[07.03. Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering 🎯|enlightenment]] and "unwholesome" means any action that moves *away from* enlightenment. 

I prefer "skilful" because it connotes a choice rather than the sense of moral arbitration that comes with the general English understanding of "right". We can cultivate habits that are more skilful than others, giving us #agency in the #experience and #perception of #reality: where habitually unskilful actions of mind, body and speech once caused suffering in our experience, cultivating skilful habits moves us toward consistent and sustainable happiness, and away from bog-standard suffering.

# Skilful View, *sammā-diṭṭhi*
**aka** Skilful Understanding; Right View

It makes sense that I should open this Path factor first, considering it is the one aspect of the Eightfold Path I have had most trouble with, as reported in my experience at the Tibetan centre in [[00. Introduction#^80ca36|Adelaide]]. Skilful view is also usually presented first on the Path, and there are reasons for that: without faith in the views taught here, it is unlikely a practitioner can make serious progress. 

It has also been important for me to recognise it is not quite true that one can be Buddhist without holding certain beliefs, because right view includes belief in rebirth, at least, and also kamma - it's quite a [[#^gnarly-view|gnarly list]], between mundane and supramundane view. Coming to terms with this has been helpful, because I think I was experiencing a generalised reluctance to take things on faith because I had thought I wouldn't need to. 

A great appeal of Buddhism for many Westerners is that one doesn't have to take on beliefs they cannot prove before they can start practising Buddhism, which I think is related to our [[01. The Nature of Doubt#Fear of Credulity|fear of credulity]]. A whole Secular Buddhism movement has developed around this, and I could relate to it for a while because I didn't like the idea of *just accepting* beliefs I couldn't understand or prove to be justified. But I have since learnt that Buddhism requires a different kind of faith, which is more like a confidence in these beliefs, lightly held while one puts those beliefs to the test - so we *can* justify them, and Buddha never asked us to accept them without endeavouring to do so. 

To do so we need to start by understanding what beliefs are expected of us. 

🎯 A gnarly one that I have always had trouble with, and which probably causes trouble for anyone approaching this practice with a desire to not be told what to believe. There are two levels of skilful view, mundane ([[MN 117 Mahācattārīsaka Sutta, 'The Great Forty'|MN 117]]) and supramundane ([[MN 9 Sammādiṭṭhisutta, 'Right View'|MN 9]]), which feature respectively: ^gnarly-view

- generosity is merited
- actions have consequences (*kamma*)
- there is this world, and another world (rebirth)
- there are mothers and fathers - and they are to be forever held with gratitude because they enabled this precious human rebirth
- there are spontaneously-born beings (realms)
- there are people who have seen these truths directly

and

- the Four Truths ([[SN 45.8]])
	- suffering, the origin of suffering, the cessation of suffering, and the way to cessation
- of conditionality
	- ageing and death, its origins, cessation, and way to cessation
	- birth, 
	- the taints,
	- etc., 
	- the cycle of the whole mass of suffering, which can be leveraged for [[08. Dependent Liberation|dependent liberation]]
- and of the roots of wholesome and unwholesome conduct
- understanding of the nutriments, 
	- nutriments, the origin of nutriments, the cessation of nutriment, and the way leading to the cessation of nutriment - the nutriments being
		- physical food, gross or subtle
		- contact
		- mental volition
		- consciousness

The mundane aspects of skilful view are described in [[MN 117 Mahācattārīsaka Sutta, 'The Great Forty'|MN 117]] as being views that are 'accompanied by defilements, partaking of good deeds, and ripening in attachments', which throws me a lot because they seem contradictory. %%In suttas like MN 60, various aspects of skilful view are enumerated and I will try to describe my understanding of these below: %%

The full passage is: 

> There is meaning in giving, sacrifice, and offerings. There are fruits and results of good and bad deeds. There is an afterlife. There are such things as mother and father, and beings that are reborn spontaneously. And there are ascetics and brahmins who are rightly comported and rightly practiced, and who describe the afterlife after realising it with their own insight.’

Supramundane skilful view is described as 'noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path', because one has seen the Four Truths directly and no longer has any doubt about the Path. 'This is called right view that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path.'

An analogy might be that mundane right view is like running a program without reading the source code or documentation: I take it on reasonable confidence that the program does what it says I will do, endeavour to use the program correctly, depending on faith that it's good code; supramundane right view is having read the source code. With the supramundane view of a stream-enterer, one sees the documentation was never wrong, but no longer relies solely on faith because they have seen the truth directly.

### Rebirth

In [[MN 60 Apaṇṇaka Sutta, 'A Safe Bet'|MN 60]], the Buddha claims, very explicitly, "Since there actually is another world, one who holds the view ‘there is no other world’ has wrong view." In lieu of having our own direct experience of this metaphysical truth claim, it is advised in MN 60 that a wise or skilful person would *assume* it to be true, just in case - if it isn't true and there is actually no other world, then at least one's #wholesome motivation yields the fruit of happiness and welfare in this lifetime. Such an argument seems to be essentially consistent with [[Pascal’s Wager]]. I find these grounds to be sufficient for choosing to believe in rebirth, so this aspect, at least, of skilful view, gets a green stamp for epistemic status. 

I also happen to believe in rebirth primarily because I came into this world with affinities I can't explain without reference to previous existence. I also have no known good reason to disbelieve the various accounts of rebirth.

It may be argued that rebirth can be interpreted [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Psychological Interpretation|psychologically]] or existentially and doesn't require cosmological [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Literal Interpretation|belief in literal realms]], but I wonder how that would impact one's practice: [[00.01. Faith and Effort|whether it would undermine effort]] or faith because one would believe they are not coming back for another life and/or that we escape from samsara upon death. If our consequences end with death, there is little motivation for ethical behaviour beyond how it supports a happy current life - the possibility of eternal discontent at least, and possibly rebirth in lower realms, becomes an incentive according to [[Pascal’s Wager]]: if there is such a possibility, the best bet is to assume that possibility - if it turns out there isn't an afterlife, the least you get is a wholesome current life.

Buddha had his own version of Pascal's Wager, found in [[MN 60 Apaṇṇaka Sutta, 'A Safe Bet'|MN 60]], which is translated literally as 'A Safe Bet' by Bhikkhu Bodhi. 

Personally this reasoning is sufficient for me to assume rebirth is real, and other aspects of Buddhist cosmology make more sense to me than the Big Ban one-universe theory, which is not a question of skilful view, and is discussed [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#Metaphysics|elsewhere]].

# Skilful Aspiration, *sammā-saṅkappa*
**aka** Skilful Intention; Skilful Attitude; Skilful Thought

A common misconception of Buddhism is that there is no place for desire ~ because desire causes suffering, right? Not quite. A healthy understanding of *sammā-saṅkappa* is where wholesome desire (*chanda*) fits into the Path. The word *tanha* denotes "desire arising out of ignorance", whereas *chanda* denotes something more like "skilful desire" such as the desire to realise the cessation of suffering.
# Skilful Speech, *sammā-vācā*
**aka** Skilful Verbal Conduct

✅

Skilful speech is the practise of respecting truth, and avoiding harsh speech, gossip and idle talk. The practise of skilful speech is of such importance that it features in the first [[#the Five Precepts]] of any dharma practitioner. The four classic formulations are: 

1. respect truthfulness and try to avoid false speech ~ lying and deceit
2. be kind and inclusive and try to avoid divisive speech
3. be gentle and avoid harsh and vulgar speech
4. respect your energy and avoid indulging in idle chatter or gossip

As with other actions (of body and mind), verbal actions have the power to harm or heal and the above four points are just guidelines.
