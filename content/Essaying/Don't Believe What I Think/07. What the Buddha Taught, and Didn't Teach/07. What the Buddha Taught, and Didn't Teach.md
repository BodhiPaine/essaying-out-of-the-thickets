# What the Buddha Taught
An important aspect of cultivating *saddhā*, hopefully obviously, is understanding what the Buddha taught, and didn't teach. We need to understand who the Buddha was, what he taught, and how to practice in pursuit of the spiritual development he described, from full-blown ignorant worldling through the four stages of awakening to fully awakened arahant. 

We can start with a simple but extraordinarily helpful teaching found in the somewhat famous [[MN 20 Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta, 'The Removal of Distracting Thoughts']], about how to deal with distracting thoughts. Can you imagine the thrill of #relief I felt upon learning there was a whole sutta dedicated to this?! And then! I later learnt there is something called [[#The Thought Trilogy]], with MN 18 and MN 19 both dealing with this subject. Thought Trilogy, I love it. 

But also, to get our priorities straight we can start with Buddha's exhortation that we pursue [[#Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering|full awakening]] in this lifetime. 



## The Three Marks
All conditioned phenomena are characterised by impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and non-self.
### *Anicca*, impermanence
All conditioned things are impermanent, subject to change, alterable - and therefore unreliable as a source of sustainable happiness.
### *Dukkha*, unsatisfactoriness
All conditioned things are unsatisfactory, and therefore pursuing them and expecting satisfaction is deluded and unreliable.
### *Anattā*, non-self 
All conditioned things are not-self, including the thing we think is our self. 

In his chapter on 'the doctrine of no-soul' (==anatta==), [[What the Buddha Taught by Walpola Rahula|Rahula]] refutes a variety of scholastic attempts to argue that Buddha did not deny the existence of a capital-S Self or soul, even though he may have denied the reality of the relative or mundane self, small-s self. These arguments might[^5] be a good starting point for anyone questioning the Buddha’s claim about the non-existence of a permanent or absolute 'true self'. They also might not - I found them a bit murky.

A good starting point for me is recognising I may need to unpack how my #faith is impacted by considerations of whether there is more to the self than Buddha understood. Maybe modern science or neuroscience has discovered or made a convincing [[metaphysics#eternalism|case that there *is* an essential soul or aspect of our being that is unchanging]]? So I checked, and actually neuroscience has confirmed Buddha's insight more than challenged it, supporting the understanding that *the self is a process* not a thing. ==[citations]==

Still there is the concern of [[#Psychotherapy|whether practising dhamma works at the level of personality]], and if not, which modalities do I need to [[01. The Nature of Doubt#Is Buddhism Holistic?|complement the practice]]? What sort of [[psychological technology]] might amount to a complementary medicine to the Buddha's dhamma? # What the Buddha Didn't Teach
## Psychotherapy
It has been helpful for me to learn that even *becoming an arahant doesn't operate on the level of the #personality* ([[Bodhi]], p. 16), because meeting purported arahants whose behaviour was uninspiring to say the least, would sometimes leave me doubting the sangha *and* the dhamma: *If these are the results, I'm not interested.* 

There are numerous examples of apparently enlightened individuals who behave poorly, even unethically, and who are even unlikeable because their personalities were not impacted by their awakening, and this raises a separate question for me, which is [[whether Buddhism is as holistic as I thought]] or as comprehensive as I might want it to be, or whether the work of developing the heart-mind with Buddhist practice needs to be complemented by modern/Western work on developing #wholeness as well as #wholesomeness in their personality.

This is relevant to #faith because the fact he didn't teach psychotherapy doesn't undermine/misrepresent the efficacy of the dhamma and just means it is worthwhile complementing one's dhamma practice with things like shadow work.

Although one may become more compassionate, gentle, etc. because of the training, it is the training that causes this not the awakening itself, and an arahant may not necessarily iron out all the habits we may see as character flaws, like gruffness or aloofness or low emotional intelligence/awareness. This doesn't mean the dhamma isn't working, just that the dhamma operates on a level other than the personality. 

Arguing against the view that we can discount cosmological facets such like [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#Rebirth|rebirth]] as "religious trappings of an ancient culture utterly inappropriate for the Dhamma of our technological age", Bhikkhu Bodhi writes in his short piece "[Dhamma Without Rebirth](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/bps-essay_06.html)" that when we

> downplay the doctrine of rebirth and explain the entire import of the Dhamma as the amelioration of mental suffering through enhanced self-awareness \[we] deprive the Dhamma of those wider perspectives from which it derives its full breadth and profundity. By doing so one seriously risks reducing it in the end to little more than a sophisticated ancient system of humanistic psychotherapy. #panoramic-persepective 

#progress We cannot advance to the higher levels of training without well-developed *saddhā* in right view. "Our motivation acquires the requisite maturity by the cultivation of right view, the first factor of the Noble Eightfold Path, which as explained by the Buddha includes an understanding of the principles of kamma and rebirth as fundamental to the structure of our existence."[^4]

Nonetheless, I consider there to be value in considering [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Psychological Interpretation|the psychological interpretation]], as an entry point at least. It is quite a leap to go from there to a full-blown [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Literal Interpretation|literal interpretation]], but I am happy to lean-in to that because I find the [[panoramic perspective]] consoling I suppose - that doesn't sound very scientific, but it starts with the idea that our whole present is nested in causes and conditions that are largely out of our control ... that takes the pressure off, somehow; and if rebirth is real I have infinite changes - this one 'dress rehearsal' isn't my only shot. %%link this with Kundera%% 
## Metaphysics

> [!NOTE] Scope
> Buddhist practice may not lead us to insight into the nature of all reality, but it leads us to direct insight into the nature of the mind - which might be the same thing. These limitations impact faith for me, because if I want certain answers that Buddhism doesn't address then I need to either abandon those questions or complement my practice with other modalities of esoteric inquiry/investigation, such as shadow work.

Reading [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] has helped me understand more clearly what I already suspected, which is that 🎯 not all spiritual practices 'point to the same underlying reality', and the 'esoteric doctrines found within every religious tradition are not all derived from the same insights'. I would add that neither does science - for all its powers in certain areas, there is no confirmed theory about the nature of reality. %%Is there?%% So it's not like I need to abandon science altogether - it just happens to be limited in its scope, and I need to adjust my expectations, the same as I need to adjust my expectations of Buddhism, knowing that all of these traditions are limited in what they investigate. ^Harris-nature-of-reality

I suppose Buddha knew (as he stated many times) that by teaching the truth of the end of suffering he necessarily wasn't teaching, or making claims about, other truths, such as metaphysical truths. Knowing the limitations of [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach|what the Buddha taught]] is going to support one's practice if one is curious about things like metaphysics - it will help to know which curiosities do not lead to [[07.03. Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering 🎯|liberation]]. 

One such curio: *How does Buddhist meditation [[07.02. Can Dhamma Save the World?|create change in society]]?* This can become a source of doubt that can creep in easily, under the [[01. The Nature of Doubt#I Should be 'Helping'|I should be helping]] guise, if one is not clear on how getting one's own house in order first is always the priority, the way of [[06. Cultivating Saddhā#For the Welfare and Happiness of Many, Gods and Humans|supporting the welfare of gods and humans]].

One benefit of spiritual practice though, is that where science relies on abstraction[^6] to describe reality, spiritual practice (or 'psychological technologies') gives the individual direct access to certain aspects of reality that is more empirical than the abstractions of science. Although the teachings generally rely on abstractions, their intention is to catalyse the insights of spiritual practice that can be experienced directly by any individual who puts in the right effort -  *paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhīti*, (able) 'to be individually ascertained by the wise' is one of the six qualities of the dhamma that we contemplate in *[[06. Cultivating Saddhā#Dhammānusati|dhammānusati]]*. 

Harris writes (20) his version of this, about how individuals 'of all faiths have glimpsed many of the same truths', and that 'any view of consciousness and the cosmos that is available to the human mind can, in principle, be appreciated by anyone'.

Anyone having such an experience would find it difficult to articulate their findings to another, but they are individually transformed, and their alternative way of being inspires others to enquire about how they might apply the same teachings that lead to those findings and a similar transformation. This is how our practice influences society. %%What is the word in science when findings can be repeatedly reproduced?%% 

Scientific method is not easy to teach, and most of us don't have access to the sort of technology required to enter the game. The insights or conclusions vary depending on which abstraction the current scientist relied on. And the scope is limited, partly by ideology and partly by technology, but mostly by the simple truth that not everything can be observed at once - we can never know the whole directly, and that is not a limit of metaphysics, just of reality itself. The classic Zen garden gracefully refers to this. 

So neither science nor Buddhism or any other spiritual practice leads to an understanding of ultimate reality - that is my current position. But what Buddhism does offer, as [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] writes (20), is a 'truly sophisticated, empirical approach to understanding the human mind'. In Buddhism we have the idea that the whole world is (within) the fathom-long body and the mind is the forerunner of all states: if so, then understanding the mind equals understanding all reality? 

I really need to explore this more - get my head around it, so to speak. I would confidently say at least, that such understanding of the mind was the explicit limit of Buddha's scope: he taught an understanding that would lead to liberation from mind-made suffering; he didn't speak about other aspects of metaphysics because they didn't serve this goal - whether he knew about those aspects or not is irrelevant. 

All that's relevant is whether Buddhism addresses the concerns I have or ~solves the problems~ I think we need to solve. This is where my faith is sometimes impacted, because for example, Buddhism doesn't promise to operate on the level of the personality - as I said above, meeting purported arahants whose behaviour was uninspiring to say the least, would sometimes leave me doubting the sangha *and* the dhamma: *If these are the results, I'm not interested.* 

That he didn't teach psychotherapy doesn't undermine or misrepresent the efficacy of the dhamma - it just means  I need to complement my practice with modalities that do take a healthy self as their end goal, because I believe such a [[healthy sense of self]] is required for transcendence.

If I find out that Buddhism also doesn't address metaphysical questions I think are important, that is less a limitation of Buddhism than a limitation of my own capacity to curb my curiosity. 

Circling back to the spiritual experiences that anyone can have, which Harris describes as *psychological* experiences and Buddha described as *paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhī ti*, 'to be individually ascertained by the wise', and the fact that such experiences can be replicated - it is my hope, from this, that we limited humans will continue developing a form of something like transpersonal and experiential psychology that will help us transcend the differences of interpretation that result from divergent metaphysical worldviews. 

Any individual can have these experiences with appropriate training and guidance, and I suppose that dualistic or monistic beliefs become irrelevant in the direct experience of our shared ground. [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] (21) discredits Aldous Huxley's Leibnizian perennial philosophy on the grounds that Abrahamic religion is dualistic while Eastern traditions transcend duality, which is not enough to convince me that there isn't common ground in some form of universal experience - what Huxley called the 'highest common factor'.

But meanwhile I need to get my own house in order, which includes limiting myself to those areas of metaphysics that make the difference between suffering and flourishing.
