# What the Buddha Taught
An important aspect of cultivating *saddhā*, hopefully obviously, is understanding what the Buddha taught, and didn't teach. We need to understand who the Buddha was, what he taught, and how to practice in pursuit of the spiritual development he described, from full-blown ignorant worldling through the four stages of awakening to fully awakened arahant. 

We can start with a simple but extraordinarily helpful teaching found in the somewhat famous [[MN 20 Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta, 'The Removal of Distracting Thoughts']], about how to deal with distracting thoughts. Can you imagine the thrill of #relief I felt upon learning there was a whole sutta dedicated to this?! And then! I later learnt there is something called [[#The Thought Trilogy]], with MN 18 and MN 19 both dealing with this subject. Thought Trilogy, I love it. 

But also, to get our priorities straight we can start with Buddha's exhortation that we pursue [[#Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering|full awakening]] in this lifetime. 




## The Four Noble Truths
There is suffering, a cause for suffering, an end to the cause for suffering ~ and that end is [[#the Eightfold Path]].

These truths are what the Buddha is said to have realised upon his awakening, and they are at the core of what he taught. They are called "noble" because understanding them (non-conceptually, but with penetrative insight) results in the highest state of virtue and happiness, and Buddhism is ultimately about improving our conduct and being happy or content with reality as it is. 

The Four Noble Truths are: 

- [[#There is Suffering|there is suffering]] (*dukkha ariya-sacca*)
- [[#There is a Cause for Suffering|there is a cause for suffering]] (*dukkha-samudaya ariya-sacca*)
- [[#There is an End to the Cause of Suffering|there is an end to the cause of suffering]] (*dukkha-nirodha ariya-sacca*)
- and the end to the cause of suffering is [[#the Eightfold Path|the Noble Eightfold Path]] (*dukkha-nirodha-gāminī paṭipadā ariya-sacca*)

When we say suffering in this context, it is honestly not as bad as it sounds ~ it is a misconception that the Buddha taught that all life is suffering. [[What is Suffering?|What, then, is suffering?]]

#### the Eightfold Path
*a set of non-linear instructions for conduct that moves one toward liberation* 

When Buddha realised the Four Noble Truths, that there is suffering, a cause of suffering, an end to the cause of suffering ... the fourth Noble Truth was that the Noble Eightfold Path is the end to the cause of suffering. The eight Path factors are 

1. Skillful[^7] View (*sammā-diṭṭhi*)
2. Skillful Aspiration (*sammā-sankappa*)
3. Skillful Speech (*sammā-vācā*)
4. Skillful Action (*sammā-kammanta*)
5. Skillful Livelihood (*samma-ājīva*)
6. Skillful Effort (*sammā-vayama*)
7. Skillful Mindfulness (*sammā-sati*)
8. Skillful Concentration (*sammā-samādhi*)
9. Skillful Consumerism

Okay, so that's 9 - and there are also two other Path factors that are experienced at fruition of the Eightfold Path, being Skilful Knowledge and Skilful Liberation. This is called the Noble Tenfold Path, and because I value the consideration of contemporary scholars that we need to include Skilful Consumerism at least in our own practice, if not as a doctrinal appendage, I have started rendering the Path as the nFold Noble Path.
##### A Note on Translation
The Pali word *sammā* (Sanskrit, *śamyā*) translates as "right", "correct", "wholesome", "appropriate" or "skilful", where "wholesome" means any action that moves *toward* [[#Full Awakening, or Liberation from Suffering 🎯|enlightenment]] and "unwholesome" means any action that moves *away from* enlightenment. 

I prefer "skilful" because it connotes a choice rather than the sense of moral arbitration that comes with the general English understanding of "right". We can cultivate habits that are more skilful than others, giving us #agency in the #experience and #perception of #reality: where habitually unskilful actions of mind, body and speech once caused suffering in our experience, cultivating skilful habits moves us toward consistent and sustainable happiness, and away from bog-standard suffering.

##### Skilful View,* sammā-diṭṭhi*
**aka** Skilful Understanding; Right View

It makes sense that I should open this Path factor first, considering it is the one aspect of the Eightfold Path I have had most trouble with, as reported in my experience at the Tibetan centre in Adelaide.%%link to anchored paragraph%% Skilful view is also usually presented first on the Path, and there are reasons for that: without faith in the views taught here, it is unlikely a practitioner can make serious progress. 

It has also been important for me to recognise it is not quite true that one can be Buddhist without holding certain beliefs, because right view includes belief in rebirth, at least, and also kamma - it's quite a [[#^gnarly-view|gnarly list]], between mundane and supramundane view. Coming to terms with this has been helpful, because I think I was experiencing a generalised reluctance to take things on faith because I had thought I wouldn't need to. 

A great appeal of Buddhism for many Westerners is that one doesn't have to take on beliefs they cannot prove before they can start practising Buddhism, which I think is related to our [[01. A whole Secular Buddhism movement has developed around this, and I could relate to it for a while because I didn't like the idea of *just accepting* beliefs I couldn't understand or prove to be justified. But I have since learnt that Buddhism requires a different kind of faith, which is more like a confidence in these beliefs, lightly held while one puts those beliefs to the test - so we *can* justify them, and Buddha never asked us to accept them without endeavouring to do so. 

To do so we need to start by understanding what beliefs are expected of us.

The mundane aspects of skilful view are described in [[MN 117 Mahācattārīsaka Sutta, 'The Great Forty'|MN 117]] as being views that are 'accompanied by defilements, partaking of good deeds, and ripening in attachments', which throws me a lot because they seem contradictory. %%In suttas like MN 60, various aspects of skilful view are enumerated and I will try to describe my understanding of these below: %%

> There is meaning in giving, sacrifice, and offerings. There are fruits and results of good and bad deeds. There is an afterlife. Supramundane skilful view is described as 'noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path', because one has seen the Four Truths directly and no longer has any doubt about the Path. 'This is called right view that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path.'

An analogy might be that mundane right view is like running a program without reading the source code or documentation: I take it on reasonable confidence that the program does what it says I will do, endeavour to use the program correctly, depending on faith that it's good code; supramundane right view is having read the source code. In [[MN 60 Apaṇṇaka Sutta, 'A Safe Bet'|MN 60]], the Buddha claims, very explicitly, "Since there actually is another world, one who holds the view ‘there is no other world’ has wrong view." In lieu of having our own direct experience of this metaphysical truth claim, it is advised in MN 60 that a wise or skilful person would *assume* it to be true, just in case - if it isn't true and there is actually no other world, then at least one's #wholesome motivation yields the fruit of happiness and welfare in this lifetime. Such an argument seems to be essentially consistent with [[Pascal’s Wager]]. I find these grounds to be sufficient for choosing to believe in rebirth, so this aspect, at least, of skilful view, gets a green stamp for epistemic status. 

I also happen to believe in rebirth primarily because I came into this world with affinities I can't explain without reference to previous existence. I also have no known good reason to disbelieve the various accounts of rebirth.

It may be argued that rebirth can be interpreted [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Psychological Interpretation|psychologically]] or existentially and doesn't require cosmological [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Literal Interpretation|belief in literal realms]], but I wonder how that would impact one's practice: [[00.01. Faith and Effort|whether it would undermine effort]] or faith because one would believe they are not coming back for another life and/or that we escape from samsara upon death. If our consequences end with death, there is little motivation for ethical behaviour beyond how it supports a happy current life - the possibility of eternal discontent at least, and possibly rebirth in lower realms, becomes an incentive according to [[Pascal’s Wager]]: if there is such a possibility, the best bet is to assume that possibility - if it turns out there isn't an afterlife, the least you get is a wholesome current life.

Buddha had his own version of Pascal's Wager, found in [[MN 60 Apaṇṇaka Sutta, 'A Safe Bet'|MN 60]], which is translated literally as 'A Safe Bet' by Bhikkhu Bodhi. 

Personally this reasoning is sufficient for me to assume rebirth is real, and other aspects of Buddhist cosmology make more sense to me than the Big Ban one-universe theory, which is not a question of skilful view, and is discussed [[#Metaphysics|elsewhere]].

#### Skilful Aspiration

A common misconception of Buddhism is that there is no place for desire ~ because desire causes suffering, right? Not quite. A healthy understanding of *sammā-saṅkappa* is where wholesome desire (*chanda*) fits into the Path. The word *tanha* denotes "desire arising out of ignorance", whereas *chanda* denotes something more like "skilful desire" such as the desire to realise the cessation of suffering.
#### Skilful Speech
*sammā-vācā*
**aka** Skilful Verbal Conduct

✅

Skilful speech is the practise of respecting truth, and avoiding harsh speech, gossip and idle talk. The practise of skilful speech is of such importance that it features in the first [[#the Five Precepts]] of any dharma practitioner. The four classic formulations are: 

1. respect truthfulness and try to avoid false speech ~ lying and deceit
2. be kind and inclusive and try to avoid divisive speech
3. be gentle and avoid harsh and vulgar speech
4. respect your energy and avoid indulging in idle chatter or gossip

As with other actions (of body and mind), verbal actions have the power to harm or heal and the above four points are just guidelines.

## The Three Marks
All conditioned phenomena are characterised by impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and non-self.
### *Anicca*, impermanence
All conditioned things are impermanent, subject to change, alterable - and therefore unreliable as a source of sustainable happiness.
### *Dukkha*, unsatisfactoriness
All conditioned things are unsatisfactory, and therefore pursuing them and expecting satisfaction is deluded and unreliable.
### *Anattā*, non-self 
All conditioned things are not-self, including the thing we think is our self. 

In his chapter on 'the doctrine of no-soul' (==anatta==), [[What the Buddha Taught by Walpola Rahula|Rahula]] refutes a variety of scholastic attempts to argue that Buddha did not deny the existence of a capital-S Self or soul, even though he may have denied the reality of the relative or mundane self, small-s self. These arguments might[^5] be a good starting point for anyone questioning the Buddha’s claim about the non-existence of a permanent or absolute 'true self'. They also might not - I found them a bit murky.

# What the Buddha Didn't Teach
## Psychotherapy
It has been helpful for me to learn that even *becoming an arahant doesn't operate on the level of the #personality* ([[Bodhi]], p. 16), because meeting purported arahants whose behaviour was uninspiring to say the least, would sometimes leave me doubting the sangha *and* the dhamma: *If these are the results, I'm not interested.* 

There are numerous examples of apparently enlightened individuals who behave poorly, even unethically, and who are even unlikeable because their personalities were not impacted by their awakening, and this raises a separate question for me, which is [[whether Buddhism is as holistic as I thought]] or as comprehensive as I might want it to be, or whether the work of developing the heart-mind with Buddhist practice needs to be complemented by modern/Western work on developing #wholeness as well as #wholesomeness in their personality.

This is relevant to #faith because the fact he didn't teach psychotherapy doesn't undermine/misrepresent the efficacy of the dhamma and just means it is worthwhile complementing one's dhamma practice with things like shadow work.

Although one may become more compassionate, gentle, etc. because of the training, it is the training that causes this not the awakening itself, and an arahant may not necessarily iron out all the habits we may see as character flaws, like gruffness or aloofness or low emotional intelligence/awareness. This doesn't mean the dhamma isn't working, just that the dhamma operates on a level other than the personality. 

Arguing against the view that we can discount cosmological facets such like [[07. What the Buddha Taught, and Didn't Teach#Rebirth|rebirth]] as "religious trappings of an ancient culture utterly inappropriate for the Dhamma of our technological age", Bhikkhu Bodhi writes in his short piece "[Dhamma Without Rebirth](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/bps-essay_06.html)" that when we

> downplay the doctrine of rebirth and explain the entire import of the Dhamma as the amelioration of mental suffering through enhanced self-awareness \[we] deprive the Dhamma of those wider perspectives from which it derives its full breadth and profundity. By doing so one seriously risks reducing it in the end to little more than a sophisticated ancient system of humanistic psychotherapy. #panoramic-persepective 

#progress We cannot advance to the higher levels of training without well-developed *saddhā* in right view. "Our motivation acquires the requisite maturity by the cultivation of right view, the first factor of the Noble Eightfold Path, which as explained by the Buddha includes an understanding of the principles of kamma and rebirth as fundamental to the structure of our existence."[^4]

Nonetheless, I consider there to be value in considering [[05. Tripping Over Worldviews#The Psychological Interpretation|the psychological interpretation]], as an entry point at least. It is quite a leap to go from there to a full-blown [[05. ## Metaphysics

> [!NOTE] Scope
> Buddhist practice may not lead us to insight into the nature of all reality, but it leads us to direct insight into the nature of the mind - which might be the same thing. These limitations impact faith for me, because if I want certain answers that Buddhism doesn't address then I need to either abandon those questions or complement my practice with other modalities of esoteric inquiry/investigation, such as shadow work.

Reading [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] has helped me understand more clearly what I already suspected, which is that 🎯 not all spiritual practices 'point to the same underlying reality', and the 'esoteric doctrines found within every religious tradition are not all derived from the same insights'. I would add that neither does science - for all its powers in certain areas, there is no confirmed theory about the nature of reality. %%Is there?%% So it's not like I need to abandon science altogether - it just happens to be limited in its scope, and I need to adjust my expectations, the same as I need to adjust my expectations of Buddhism, knowing that all of these traditions are limited in what they investigate. ^Harris-nature-of-reality

One benefit of spiritual practice though, is that where science relies on abstraction[^6] to describe reality, spiritual practice (or 'psychological technologies') gives the individual direct access to certain aspects of reality that is more empirical than the abstractions of science. Although the teachings generally rely on abstractions, their intention is to catalyse the insights of spiritual practice that can be experienced directly by any individual who puts in the right effort - for this reason, one of the qualities of the dhamma is *paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhī ti*, (able) 'to be individually ascertained by the wise. Harris writes (20) about how individuals 'of all faiths have glimpsed many of the same truths', and that 'any view of consciousness and the cosmos that is available to the human mind can, in principle, be appreciated by anyone'. ==The suttas report something similar.==
- [ ] find where it says in the suttas that anyone with the five faculties and the six senses has the same potential to become enlightened, and %% there may be good examples in William James' Varieties of Religious Experience%%

Anyone having such an experience would find it difficult to articulate their findings to another, but they are individually transformed, and their alternative way of being inspires others to enquire about how they might apply the same teachings that lead to those findings and a similar transformation. %%What is the word in science when findings can be repeatedly reproduced?%% 

Scientific method is not easy to teach, and most of us don't have access to the sort of technology required to enter the game. The insights or conclusions vary depending on which abstraction the current scientist relied on. And the scope is limited, partly by ideology and partly by technology, but mostly by the simple truth that not everything can be observed at once - we can never know the whole directly, and that is not a limit of metaphysics, just of reality itself. The classic Zen garden gracefully refers to this. 

So neither science nor Buddhism or any other spiritual practice leads to an understanding of ultimate reality - that is my current position. But what Buddhism does offer, as [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] writes (20), is a 'truly sophisticated, empirical approach to understanding the human mind'. In Buddhism we have the idea that the whole world is (within) the fathom-long body and the mind is the forerunner of all states: if so, then understanding the mind equals understanding all reality? 

I really need to explore this more - get my head around it, so to speak. I would confidently say at least, that such understanding of the mind was the explicit limit of Buddha's scope: he taught an understanding that would lead to liberation from mind-made suffering; he didn't speak about other aspects of metaphysics because they didn't serve this goal - whether he knew about those aspects or not is irrelevant. 

All that's relevant is whether Buddhism addresses the concerns I have or ~solves the problems~ I think we need to solve. This is where my faith is sometimes impacted, because for example, Buddhism doesn't promise to operate on the level of the personality - as I said above, meeting purported arahants whose behaviour was uninspiring to say the least, would sometimes leave me doubting the sangha *and* the dhamma: *If these are the results, I'm not interested.* 

That he didn't teach psychotherapy doesn't undermine or misrepresent the efficacy of the dhamma - it just means  I need to complement my practice with modalities that do take a healthy self as their end goal, because I believe such a [[healthy sense of self]] is required for transcendence.

If I find out that Buddhism also doesn't address metaphysical questions I think are important, that is less a limitation of Buddhism than a limitation of my own capacity to curb my curiosity. 

Circling back to the spiritual experiences that anyone can have, which Harris describes as *psychological* experiences and Buddha described as *paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhī ti*, 'to be individually ascertained by the wise', and the fact that such experiences can be replicated - it is my hope, from this, that we limited humans will continue developing a form of something like transpersonal and experiential psychology that will help us transcend the differences of interpretation that result from divergent metaphysical worldviews. 

Any individual can have these experiences with appropriate training and guidance, and I suppose that dualistic or monistic beliefs become irrelevant in the direct experience of our shared ground. [[Waking Up, Harris|Harris]] (21) discredits Aldous Huxley's Leibnizian perennial philosophy on the grounds that Abrahamic religion is dualistic while Eastern traditions transcend duality, which is not enough to convince me that there isn't common ground in some form of universal experience - what Huxley called the 'highest common factor'.

But meanwhile I need to get my own house in order, which includes limiting myself to those areas of metaphysics that make the difference between suffering and flourishing.
